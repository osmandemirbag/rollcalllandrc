\begin{abstract}
\noindent How do landed interests act inside a democratic parliament? Sociological accounts expect deputies who own land, or who represent the countryside, to defend it with their votes, so that the conflict over land appears as a division among legislators and redistribution is defeated on the floor. Using a novel record of every named vote on land and agriculture in the Turkish parliament from 1945 to 1980---511 roll calls and 216,315 individual votes, linked to the biographies of the deputies who cast them and to the debates on the decisive land laws---I examine three aspects of landed action: deputies' positions, the form their opposition took, and the conditions under which land was redistributed. Deputies who owned or worked land, and those who represented rural provinces, voted exactly like their party colleagues; their positions followed their party's relation to the cabinet, reversing when it entered or left government. Landed opposition took the form of voice in debate, selective absence from votes on land transfers, recorded opposition on land taxes, on the one land vote it could win, and once by a party that had made land its program, and exit from the governing party. Land was redistributed only when there was no competing party to exit to. I argue that in a state that administered the countryside through executive agencies, landed interests were represented through the struggle over the cabinet rather than through the vote, and that this explains both the absence of landed voting blocs and the authoritarian timing of land reform.

\medskip
\noindent\textbf{Keywords:} landed elites, agrarian politics, land reform, legislative behavior, voice and exit, Turkey
\end{abstract}

Few actors have occupied as central a place in political sociology as the owners of land. From \citet{moore1966} to \citet{paige1975} and \citet{skocpol1979}, the strength and orientation of landed classes have been treated as a key to whether societies take democratic or authoritarian paths into the modern world, and recent work has returned to the question with new evidence: landed elites shape the timing and form of democratization through the parties that represent them \citep{ziblatt2017}, and they block redistribution wherever democratic institutions give them the means to do so \citep{albertus2015}. Much of this literature rests on a premise about what landed interests do once democratic institutions exist. It assumes that they act in the open arena of representation, that deputies who own land or speak for the countryside defend it with their votes, and that the conflict over land is therefore decided by a contest between landed and other legislators.

That premise has rarely been examined directly. The influence of landed interests is usually inferred from outcomes---from reforms that were not enacted, or that were diluted---or from the statements of prominent landowners in debate and memoir. Both kinds of evidence are compatible with very different accounts of how landed interests act. A reform may fail because landed deputies vote it down, because their parties keep it off the agenda, because the government cannot afford to alienate them, or because they leave the governing party. A landowner's speech against a bill may be followed by a vote against it, by an abstention, or by a vote in its favor. To know how landed interests act in a parliament, one must observe what the deputies who hold them do: how they vote, whether they vote, and whether they stay.

This article asks how landed interests acted in the parliament of a late-developing, predominantly agrarian democracy, and examines three aspects of that action. The first is \textit{position}: whether deputies' votes on land and agriculture followed their own relation to land, the character of their constituencies, or their party's relation to the government. The second is \textit{form}: whether landed opposition to a measure took the form of a recorded vote against it, an absence from the vote, a speech, or a departure from the party. The third is \textit{outcome}: under what conditions land was in fact redistributed. Sociological theories of class and constituency, the articulation approach to parties, and political-science theories of the cabinet make different predictions about each, and the predictions can be tested with the right evidence.

Turkey provides a strategic case. Its competitive politics began in a dispute over land: the 1945 law to distribute land to farmers was debated in the months when the founders of the Democrat Party (DP), which would end single-party rule in 1950, broke with the governing Republican People's Party (CHP), and the received account treats the law as the moment when landed notables took their interests into opposition \citep{karpat1959,keyderpamuk1984}. Land reform returned to the agenda in every decade that followed---under a two-party system, under a fragmented multiparty system with a radical left in parliament, and under military tutelage---and it passed again only once, in 1973. I assembled a record of every named vote on land and agriculture in the Turkish parliament from 1945 to 1980: 511 roll calls and 216,315 individual votes, linked to the biographies of 2,979 deputies, including their occupations, and to the speeches given in the debates on the decisive land laws.

The record shows that landed interests did not act through the vote. Deputies who owned or worked land, and those who represented rural provinces, voted exactly like their party colleagues. Their positions followed their party's relation to the cabinet: the same deputies voted for and against the same farm agencies as their parties entered and left government, and deputies who left the governing party turned against its farm budgets while those who moved between opposition parties did not change their votes at all. Landed opposition took other forms. Landed deputies spoke far beyond their numbers in the debates on land; the opposition stayed away from votes on laws that transferred or registered land, recorded its opposition on land taxes, on the one land vote it could win, and once when it had made land its program, and, when the governing party would not accommodate its landed wing, that wing left. And land was redistributed only twice, both times when there was no competing party to which its opponents could exit.

I argue that these regularities reflect the way a strong state shaped the representation of landed interests. In a state that administered the countryside through executive agencies---a grain board, state farms, an irrigation authority, a land-reform administration---the resources that mattered to landowners and peasants alike flowed through the government, and every vote on agriculture was also a vote on the government that controlled them. Parties articulated agrarian conflict as a question of who held the cabinet, which I call \emph{cabinet articulation}, and landed interests acted where the vote on the cabinet did not bind them: in debate, through absence, and through exit. This account specifies when landed interests should appear as voting blocs and when they should not, explains why the most visible instrument of landed power, the recorded vote, was the one landed deputies avoided, and suggests why land reform comes so often from authoritarian or tutelary regimes: what such regimes remove is not landed votes against reform but the exit option on which landed influence in competitive parliaments rests.

\section*{THEORIES OF LANDED INTERESTS IN PARLIAMENTS}

Sociologists have increasingly called for theories of political organization that specify their historical and institutional scope conditions rather than positing universal mechanisms \citep{mudge2014}. With respect to landed interests, existing theories offer four accounts of how they act in a parliament, and two further arguments about the forms their opposition takes and the conditions under which land is redistributed. I discuss each in turn and derive its implications for the three aspects of landed action I examine: the positions deputies take, the form of their opposition, and the outcome of land legislation.

\subsection*{Class Representation}

The most direct account treats landed interests as class interests represented by those who hold them. In the comparative-historical tradition, the political behavior of landowners follows from their position in agrarian class structure: \citet{moore1966} argued that labor-repressive landed classes were the principal obstacle to democracy, and \citet{paige1975} tied the forms of rural conflict to the ways in which cultivators and noncultivators derive their income from land. In the theory of party systems, the conflict between landed and industrial interests is one of the critical cleavages from which modern parties emerged \citep{lipset1967}. More recent work traces democratization to the organization of landed elites in conservative parties \citep{ziblatt2017} and attributes the rarity of land reform under democracy to the representation of landed elites in democratic institutions \citep{albertus2015}. In a parliament, this account implies what I call the \textit{landed representation} hypothesis: deputies who own or work land should vote against the redistribution and taxation of land, and for measures that benefit landowners, regardless of party. Parties that represent landed interests should, moreover, contain more landed deputies than others. In Turkey, where no agrarian party of significance formed, the hypothesis implies that the DP and its successors should be more landed than the CHP, and that landed deputies within every party should diverge from their colleagues on land.

\subsection*{Constituency Representation}

A second account locates agrarian interests not in deputies' own holdings but in their constituencies. Deputies seeking reelection respond to the interests of those who elect them \citep{mayhew1974}, and theories of agricultural policy in developing countries hold that the representation of rural majorities depends on their capacity to act collectively against concentrated urban interests \citep{bates1981,olson1965}. In the Turkish literature, \citeauthor{mardin1973}'s (\citeyear{mardin1973}) account of a confrontation between a centralizing bureaucratic elite and a provincial periphery implies that deputies from the periphery, represented above all by the DP and its successors, carried agrarian demands into parliament. This account implies the \textit{constituency representation} hypothesis: deputies from rural provinces, or from provinces suffering agrarian distress, should support agrarian benefits and oppose burdens on agriculture more than their party colleagues, and the place from which a deputy was elected should explain part of his vote beyond his party.

\subsection*{Partisan Articulation}

The articulation approach rejects the assumption that parties reflect preexisting social interests. Parties, on this view, actively constitute the divisions they represent, politicizing some interests and suppressing others \citep{deleon2009,deleon2015,eidlin2016}. The approach was developed in part on the Turkish case, where \citet{deleon2009} and \citet{tugal2009} show how parties articulated a division between religious and secular constituencies that did not follow mechanically from social structure. For landed interests, this implies the \textit{articulated land} hypothesis: land becomes a matter of partisan voting to the extent that parties make it one, deputies follow their parties' articulation of the land question rather than their own interests, and the land votes that divide parties should be those on which party leaders have staked out positions. Articulation theory also allows for a radical flank: a party that articulates the land question more radically than others may change outcomes by supplying decisive votes or by pulling the center toward its position \citep{haines1984}. In Turkey, the Workers' Party of Turkey (TİP), which entered parliament in 1965 demanding ceilings on landholding, provides the test of a \textit{radical flank} hypothesis.

\subsection*{The Cabinet}

Political scientists offer a fourth account, in which deputies' positions on any measure follow from their party's relation to the government. In parliamentary systems, cohesive party voting developed as the cabinet monopolized the legislative agenda and as votes on government measures became votes on the government itself \citep{cox1987}; executives use procedural instruments, including the question of confidence, to make policy votes into votes on their survival \citep{huber1996}; and governing parties control what reaches the floor \citep{cox2005}. This implies the \textit{cabinet alignment} hypothesis: deputies should vote for agrarian measures when their party holds cabinet posts and against them when it does not, a deputy's vote should change when his party's relation to the cabinet changes, and the dominant division in agrarian voting should separate government from opposition. The account has a distributive corollary. Measures that distribute benefits to large and dispersed constituencies should pass with near-unanimity, since no deputy gains from opposing them on the record \citep{weingast1979}; what should divide government and opposition is not the distribution of benefits but control of the instruments through which the government distributes them.

\subsection*{The Forms of Landed Opposition}

None of these accounts says much about the form that opposition to a land measure takes, because all of them treat the recorded vote as the natural medium of legislative action. Yet deputies who disagree with their party, or opposition parties that disagree with the government, have more than one option. \citet{hirschman1970} distinguished between voice, the attempt to change an organization from within, and exit, the decision to leave it. In a parliament, voice can be exercised in debate, in committee, and in the party's internal forums; exit takes the form of leaving the party. Between them lies a third option that the recorded vote makes available and that is easy to overlook: absence. A deputy who stays away from a vote neither supports nor opposes the measure on the record.

Which option deputies choose should depend on the costs of a recorded position. Legislators seek credit for popular positions and avoid blame for unpopular ones \citep{mayhew1974,weaver1986}. A recorded vote against a measure that gives land or titles to rural claimants is a visible position against numerous constituents, while a recorded vote against a tax on land is a position against a burden. This yields the \textit{repertoire} hypothesis. Opponents of a land measure should record their opposition when the measure imposes costs on themselves or their supporters, as taxation does, or when their votes can defeat it; they should withdraw from the vote when the measure transfers land or titles to rural beneficiaries and they cannot win; they should use voice where the vote does not record them, in debate and in party forums; and they should exit when the governing party will not accommodate them. A party that has made land its program, in the sense of the articulation approach, may record its opposition even on transfers, since for such a party the position itself is the point.

\subsection*{The Conditions of Redistribution}

A final set of arguments concerns the outcome. \citet{huntington1968} saw land reform as a characteristic project of modernizing regimes insulated from landed pressure, and \citet{albertus2015} has shown that land reform is far more common under autocracy than under democracy, because democratic institutions provide landed elites with veto points. In parliamentary terms, this implies the \textit{landed veto} hypothesis: in competitive parliaments, landed deputies should defeat redistribution with their votes, and reform should pass only when they are outvoted or excluded. The repertoire hypothesis suggests a different mechanism. If landed interests in a competitive parliament act through voice, absence, and exit rather than through recorded votes, then what protects them is not their votes but the threat of taking their support to another party, which governing parties cannot ignore. Reform should then pass when that threat is removed---when there is no competing party to which landed deputies can exit. I call this the \textit{exit option} hypothesis.

\subsection*{The State and Cabinet Articulation}

These accounts are usually treated as rivals. I propose that which of them describes landed action depends on how the state allocates the resources over which agrarian conflict is fought. Where agrarian interests are organized through markets and private associations and legislation allocates agrarian resources, landed and rural deputies have reason to defend their interests with their votes, and class or constituency representation should be visible on the floor. Where the state administers the countryside through executive agencies \citep{mann1984,heper1985,keyder1987}, the resources that matter to landowners and peasants flow through the government, and every vote on agriculture is also a vote on the government that controls them. Under these conditions, parties have strong incentives to articulate agrarian conflict as a question of cabinet control, and deputies have strong incentives to follow. This is articulation in the sense of \citet{deleon2009}, but what is articulated is not a division between social groups; it is the division between those inside and outside the government. \citet{rohr2025} make an analogous argument for the formation of the first American parties, which coalesced around the organizational affordances of the state rather than around social cleavages. My concern is not with party formation but with the representation of a particular social interest, and the implication is that in such a state landed interests will be represented through the struggle for the cabinet: their positions will follow cabinet alignment, and their opposition will take the forms that the vote on the cabinet leaves open. \section*{THE CASE: TURKEY, 1945--1980}

Turkey entered this period as a single-party republic in which the CHP governed a predominantly agrarian society through a strong bureaucratic state \citep{heper1985,keyder1987}. In 1945, as the regime prepared a controlled opening to competitive politics, the government introduced the Law on Providing Land to Farmers (\textit{Çiftçiyi Topraklandırma Kanunu}, Law 4753), whose seventeenth article allowed the state to expropriate land worked by sharecroppers and tenants down to small holdings. During the debate, Celâl Bayar, Adnan Menderes, Fuat Köprülü, and Refik Koraltan, all CHP deputies, submitted a memorandum demanding political liberalization; in January 1946 they founded the DP. The standard account treats the land law as the issue on which landed notables broke with the single party, and Menderes, a landowner from the Aegean, as their spokesman \citep{karpat1959,keyderpamuk1984,zurcher2004}.

The DP won the elections of 1950 and governed for a decade, during which agricultural mechanization, rising crop prices, and an expansion of state credit transformed the countryside and tied much of the peasantry to the governing party \citep{keyder1987,ahmad1977}. The state's presence in the countryside grew with it. The Soil Products Office bought grain at supported prices, the State Farms and the Forest Administration managed large estates and forests, and from 1953 the State Hydraulic Works built irrigation; the budgets and accounts of these agencies came before parliament every year. The DP split twice: in 1948, while still in opposition, when dissidents left to found the Millet Partisi (MP), and in 1955, while in government, when dissidents expelled after a dispute over press freedom founded the Hürriyet Partisi (HP). Until 1960, deputies were elected by plurality on provincial party lists, so that a province's delegation usually belonged to a single party.

The military coup of 1960 ended DP rule. The constitution of 1961 introduced proportional representation and a second chamber, and the Justice Party (AP), heir to the DP, became the dominant party of the right. Land reform returned to the agenda. The CHP-led coalition of İsmet İnönü introduced a land reform bill in 1965; the Workers' Party of Turkey (TİP) entered parliament the same year with 15 seats and demanded ceilings on landholding; the CHP's turn toward the ``left of center'' led its right wing to leave in 1967 and found the Güven Partisi (GP); and in 1970 a group of AP deputies voted against their own government's budget and later founded the Demokratik Parti. After the military memorandum of March 12, 1971, cabinets were formed under military supervision, TİP was closed by the Constitutional Court, and in 1973 parliament passed the Land and Agrarian Reform Law (Law 1757), whose administration became a new state agency. The competitive coalitions that followed ended with the coup of 1980.

The case offers within-case variation in each of the factors the theories emphasize: the regime (single party, competitive, tutelary), the governing party (CHP, DP, AP, and coalitions), the electoral system, the presence of a radical flank, and the extent of the state's agrarian apparatus. Above all, it offers a record of individual behavior that existing accounts have not used. The power of landed notables was exercised largely through informal channels---family networks, local party organizations, committee rooms, the corridors of the Assembly---and historians have reconstructed it from memoirs, party documents, and the press, sources that tend to let a single dramatic speech stand for the behavior of many. Most agrarian policy, moreover, was made by decree or by a show of hands. The named vote is the one place where the position of every deputy on a given question was publicly recorded, and its record also preserves who was absent. It cannot reveal everything about landed power, and I do not claim that it does. But because each of the hypotheses in Table~\ref{tab:hyp} has clear implications for the named vote, for who stayed away from it, and for who left a party, the record allows them to be tested systematically and in the same way across thirty-five years.

\section*{DATA AND MEASURES}

\subsection*{Data}

\textit{Roll call votes.} The official minutes (\textit{Tutanak Dergisi}) print, for each named vote, the tallies and the full lists of deputies voting yes, no, and abstaining, of those not taking part, and of vacant seats, ordered by province. For 1961--80 I identified all named votes in the National Assembly whose subject was land, agricultural credit and prices, commodity boards, forests, irrigation, state farms, or the budgets and accounts of the agencies responsible for them. For 1943--60 I located every printed vote result in a page-level text corpus of the minutes, which yielded 1,872 named votes, of which 143 concern agriculture (1946--60) and 57 fall in the months around the 1945 land law; the latter serve as the comparison set for the land law. I read the lists from the original page images. Because the format of the lists changed over the period---the 1945 lists print initials and surnames in capitals, and the vocabulary of the sections changed from \textit{Kabul etmiyenler} and \textit{Oya katılmıyanlar} to \textit{Reddedenler} and \textit{Reye iştirak etmiyenler}---each era required its own reading, and every list was reconciled against the printed tallies. A vote is used when no section of its list holds more names than its tally; names lost to unreadable print leave a deputy's choice missing rather than miscoded. \textit{Deputies.} Deputies were linked to the Turkish Political Elites Dataset, which records every member of parliament by term, province, and party, together with characteristics of the province. Matching proceeded within term and province, by surname and initials for 1945 and by full name thereafter; 85 percent of the names on the 1945 lists and 89.5 percent of those on the 1946--60 lists were matched, and the 73 land votes of 1961--80 were verified by hand against the page images.

\subsection*{Measures}

The hypotheses concern three aspects of landed action, and each requires its own measures.

\textit{Position.} A deputy's position on a roll call is measured by his vote, coded as yes among votes cast (yes, no, or abstain), and his participation as whether he cast a vote at all. To summarize the division of positions in each period, I use the \textit{government premium}, the mean over contested roll calls of the difference between the yes shares of government and opposition deputies, and I estimate deputies' positions in a two-dimensional space for each term of the National Assembly with a Bayesian item-response model \citep{poole1997,clinton2004}.

\textit{Cabinet alignment.} A deputy is aligned with the cabinet when his party holds cabinet posts on the date of the vote; party at the vote follows each deputy's party switches by date. Alignment changes within deputies in three ways that I exploit: through the change of government in 1950, through the formation and collapse of coalitions after 1961, and through party exits.

\textit{Landed exposure.} To measure deputies' own relation to land, I coded the biographies printed in the parliament's official album, the \textit{TBMM Albümü 1920--2010}, which records each deputy's education, occupation, and career. I classify a deputy as a farmer or landowner when the biography lists farming, a farm, land, or livestock as an occupation; the 7,355 biographies for 1920--80 were matched to the elites dataset at rates between 97 and 100 percent in every term, and the share of farmers and landowners ranges from 9 percent of deputies in the 1943 Assembly to 18 percent in the 1950s. Adnan Menderes, for example, is listed as engaged in \textit{çiftçilik} (farming), and Emin Sazak, a large landowner from Eskişehir, as a \textit{ziraatçı} (agriculturalist). The biographies do not record the size of holdings, so the measure identifies deputies with a direct stake in land rather than the largest landowners alone.

\textit{Constituency exposure.} I measure the agrarian character of a deputy's constituency by the rural share of his province's population and, for 1968--80, by provincial harvest shocks, the deviation of crop yields from each province's trend. As a summary measure of constituency representation, I estimate the share of the variance of vote choices on each contested roll call that province explains beyond party.\footnote{Because province dummies explain some variance by chance---there are up to 67 provinces and a few hundred votes per roll call---I report the share explained by province beyond party net of the share explained when province labels are randomly permuted within party. Before 1961, plurality elections on provincial lists made province and party almost perfectly collinear, so the measure is informative only for 1961--80.}

\textit{Forms of opposition.} The repertoire hypothesis requires measures of each form. \textit{Voice} is measured by deputies' shares of speaker turns and of words in the land debates. \textit{Absence} is measured by a \textit{selective absence index}: for a given vote and party, the logarithm of the party's attendance on that vote divided by its own attendance on the other agrarian roll calls of the same period, so that zero indicates usual attendance and negative values indicate that the party stayed away. \textit{Recorded opposition} is the share of the party's present members who voted no. \textit{Exit} is measured by departures from a party, of which the period offers four: two from the governing party (1955 and 1970) and two between opposition parties (1948 and 1967). Finally, a party is \textit{pivotal} on a vote if it could have defeated the measure holding all other votes fixed, that is, if its no votes plus its absent members would have outnumbered the yes votes.

\textit{Validity.} Each measure has limits that bear on the tests. The occupational measure is based on how deputies or the album's editors described their occupations, and it may understate landholding among deputies who described themselves by a profession; this would bias within-party comparisons toward zero only if landed deputies who did not declare it voted differently from those who did, for which I find no indication: the measure identifies the best-known landed critics of 1945, including Menderes, Sazak, and Oral, and the null holds when the comparison is restricted to the debates and votes on land itself. The selective absence index compares a party with itself in the same period, so it is not affected by differences in attendance across parties or over time, but it cannot distinguish a deliberate withdrawal from an absence caused by scheduling; I therefore interpret it only together with the party's recorded votes on comparable measures in the same months. The pivotality measure holds all other deputies' votes fixed and so treats the opposition as acting alone; it understates pivotality when the government's own deputies were absent, and I report the outcome of each vote alongside it.

\textit{Outcome and exit availability.} The outcome is whether land legislation that transferred land to cultivators passed. Exit availability is measured by whether a competing party existed to which dissenting deputies could move, and the suspension of competition under military supervision by the distance between the yes shares of the two main parties on agrarian roll calls.

\subsection*{Designs}

I use four designs, each with an explicit estimand. First, to test landed and constituency representation, I estimate the difference between deputies with and without a given characteristic within the same party on the same roll call, using party-by-roll-call fixed effects and standard errors clustered by deputy. Second, to test cabinet alignment, I exploit changes in alignment within deputies. For 1950, I compare deputies elected in both 1946 and 1950 who remained in the DP, whose party moved from opposition to government, with those who remained in the CHP, whose party moved the other way, on the same roll calls before and after the change. For 1961--80, I use the estimator of \citet{dechaisemartin2020}, which compares deputies whose alignment switches between consecutive periods with those whose alignment does not, with a placebo for periods without switches. For the four party exits, the estimand is the difference-in-differences in yes votes between leavers and stayers before and after the split, with inference by Fisher randomization over which deputies are labelled as leavers \citep{imbens2015}. Third, to test the repertoire hypothesis, I compare the selective absence and recorded opposition of the opposition party across the key land votes of 1950--73, classified by whether the measure transferred or registered land or taxed it and by whether the opposition was pivotal. Fourth, to examine the conditions of redistribution, I trace the sequence of land legislation through process tracing \citep{collier2011}, treating the moments at which land reform reached a vote as controlled cases within a single polity \citep{slater2013}. I refer to votes by the minutes file and page on which the list appears.

\section*{ANALYSES}

\subsection*{The Division of Agrarian Positions}

I begin with the structure of positions. If landed or constituency representation organized agrarian voting, the space of positions should contain a dimension associated with deputies' land or constituencies. Figure~\ref{fig:maps} presents the estimated positions of deputies in the two National Assemblies in which the most consequential party exits occurred, 1965--69 and 1969--73. In both, the first dimension separates the governing party from the opposition, and the model classifies 97 to 98 percent of individual votes correctly. The governing AP forms a tight cluster; the opposition parties are spread out. The second dimension is unrelated to the characteristics that class and constituency accounts emphasize: its within-party correlations with the rural share of a deputy's province and with an eastern constituency are at most 0.10 in absolute value in every term.

\subsection*{Land, Constituency, and Position}

The landed representation hypothesis implies that the parties of the landed periphery should contain more landed deputies, and that landed deputies should diverge from their party colleagues on land. Neither holds. In the 1946--60 parliaments, 18.5 percent of CHP deputies were farmers or landowners, compared with 15.3 percent of DP deputies; in 1961--80, the shares were 14.5 percent for the CHP and 14.1 percent for the AP (supplement Table A2, Panel B). The party usually described as the vehicle of provincial landed interests was, if anything, slightly less landed than the party of the bureaucratic center.

They spoke against the law; they did not vote against it.

The 1950 elections provide the sharpest test, because the same deputies, who kept their party, voted on the same kinds of roll calls before and after their party's alignment was reversed. The 27 DP deputies elected in both 1946 and 1950 had voted yes on 5 percent of contested agrarian roll calls in opposition; in government, they voted yes on all of them. The 41 CHP deputies elected in both years fell from 99 percent to 40 percent. The difference-in-differences, which adds the two reversals, is 1.54, and no random reassignment of party labels among these deputies produces a value as large ($p<.001$). In the National Assembly of 1961--80, the effect of entering government on the probability of a yes vote is 0.41 (95 percent CI 0.11 to 0.50), with a placebo of 0.002 in periods without a change of alignment; entering government also raised participation by 0.22. The estimate is smaller than in 1950, plausibly because coalition partners and technocratic cabinets blurred what being in government meant, but its direction is the same across two constitutions, two electoral systems, and three decades.

The party exits test cabinet alignment against the articulated land hypothesis. A deputy whose party enters government might change his votes because his party's program changes; a party exit changes a deputy's party without necessarily changing his alignment with the cabinet. Only two of the four exits moved deputies across the line between government and opposition, and only those two changed their votes (Figure~\ref{fig:exits}). The DP deputies who left the governing party for the Hürriyet Partisi in 1955 had voted for every agrarian agency budget; afterward they voted for 48 percent, while the stayers voted for all ($-0.52$, $p<.001$). The AP deputies who left for the Demokratik Parti in 1970 fell from 100 percent to 14 percent ($-0.83$, $p<.001$). The DP deputies who moved to the Millet Partisi in 1948, and the CHP deputies who moved to the Güven Partisi in 1967, all of them in opposition before and after, changed their votes by $+0.10$ ($p=.23$) and $-0.04$ ($p=.46$). The 1967 exit is the sharpest contrast with articulation: the Güven Partisi was founded in protest against the CHP's articulation of a left-of-center program on land and other questions, yet its founders' agrarian votes did not move.

What, then, did government and opposition disagree about? The cabinet alignment hypothesis implies that the contested votes should concern the government's instruments rather than the distribution of land and benefits. They did. Of the 457 agrarian roll calls of 1946--80, 178 were contested (supplement Table A1). Budgets and final accounts of the agrarian agencies make up 64 percent of all agrarian roll calls but 84 percent of the contested ones; every one of the eight budgets of the land-reform agency created in 1973 was contested, and so were all eight votes on the taxation of land and property. By contrast, only 2 of the 14 votes on land transfers and titling, 2 of the 37 votes on farm benefits, and 1 of the 22 votes on commodity boards were contested. Under the CHP government of 1946--50, the DP opposition voted against the budgets of the agrarian agencies---the 1949 budget of the Forest Administration passed by 313 to 25 (08016054, p.~191)---but no opposition deputy voted against the distribution of seed to needy farmers, which passed by 273 to 0 (08012085, p.~103), or against the law establishing the State Farms, which passed by 251 to 1 (08020103, p.~121). Under the DP, the CHP voted against the agencies' budgets with increasing intensity---in 1959 the State Farms' budget passed by 314 to 96 and the State Hydraulic Works' by 337 to 86 (11007046, p.~175; 11007048, p.~128)---yet not one CHP deputy voted against the law establishing the State Hydraulic Works in 1953, the reorganization of the Soil Products Office in 1955, or the Sugar Law of 1956.\footnote{09026019, p.~42 (245 to 1); 10007078, p.~83 (289 to 1); 10012080, p.~31 (275 to 0). The closest the CHP came to opposing a substantive agrarian law was the Forest Law of 1956, which passed by 261 to 13; seven of the eight CHP deputies who took part, among them İnönü, voted against it (10013103, p.~59).} The agencies themselves were not in dispute. Control of them, voted on every year in their budgets, was.

The behavior of the National Salvation Party (MSP) after 1973 shows that this division owed nothing to positions on land. The MSP sat in coalition first with the CHP and then with the AP---parties at opposite ends of the land question. It voted for the budget of the Land and Agrarian Reform Undersecretariat in 1974 as the CHP's partner (45 to 0) and in 1976 and 1977 as the AP's (33 to 0 and 37 to 0), and against it in 1978 and 1979, when the CHP governed without it (0 to 11 and 0 to 4).\footnote{Counts are MSP deputies voting yes and no. The MSP also voted yes in 1975, under the caretaker cabinet of Sadi Irmak, and in 1980, when it supported Süleyman Demirel's minority government.} A party that voted for the administration of land reform with either partner, and against it when out of the cabinet, was not taking a position on land.

\subsection*{The Repertoire of Landed Opposition}

If landed interests did not act through recorded votes, how did they act? The 1945 law provides the first answer, and it corrects the received account. On May 29, 1945, two weeks before the vote on the land law, the prime minister made the seven-month budget a question of confidence. Bayar, Menderes, Koraltan, Köprülü, and Sazak cast the only five votes against it (07017065, p.~41). From that day, they stopped voting almost entirely: they were absent from 91 percent of the named votes that followed, among them the land law of June 11 (supplement Figure A1). Measured against their own absence on the other votes of those months, their absence on the land law is not distinctive ($p=.18$ over 39 votes). They did not single out the land law; they withdrew from the single party's votes in general, after recording their opposition on the one vote that was explicitly a vote on the cabinet, and returned in December 1945 only to vote against the 1946 budget.

Their opposition to the land law itself was voiced in debate. Menderes told the assembly that the bill's scheme for indivisible family farms was taken ``almost verbatim'' from the National Socialist hereditary farm law (07017055, p.~22). Cavit Oral, a landowner from Seyhan (Adana) who headed the province's farmers' union, protested that ``the provisions of Article 17 recognize no limit in land'' (07018067, p.~32). Emin Sazak was more direct: ``If I denied that my tie to land affects my speech and my feelings, I would be lying'' (07018067, p.~46). Refik Koraltan told the assembly that a unanimous vote on the law ``will be a clear answer'' to those who claimed there was opposition to it (07018069, p.~7); he then did not vote. The law passed by 345 to 0 (07018072, p.~22).

The same repertoire appears in the competitive parliaments that followed. Figure~\ref{fig:land} places each key land vote of 1950--73 according to the opposition party's selective absence and its recorded opposition. The votes fall into two groups. On four of the five land-transfer and titling bills on which the opposition was not pivotal, it withdrew: its attendance fell well below its usual level and none of its present members voted no. The DP's attendance on the CHP's 1950 dilution of the land law and its 1950 titling law, the CHP's on the DP's 1955 amendment of the land law, and the CHP's on the 1973 land reform law all fall in the lower left; in 1973, 2 of the CHP's 101 listed deputies took part (selective absence index $-3.1$). On all three land and property taxes, by contrast, the opposition attended at or above its usual level and voted no---83 percent of present AP deputies on the 1964 land tax, 96 percent of present CHP deputies on the 1970 property tax, and all present Demokratik Parti deputies on its 1972 amendment.

Three alternative explanations for the withdrawals can be set aside. First, the absences were not a general boycott of parliament: in the same periods, the same opposition parties attended agrarian votes at their usual rates and voted against the agencies' budgets, and the selective absence index measures attendance relative to that baseline. Second, they were not a matter of scheduling that happened to fall on land votes: the CHP's absence from the 1973 land reform law, when 2 of its 101 deputies took part, coincided with the AP's attendance at 89 percent of its listed members on the same day, and the CHP had attended the land-tax votes of the preceding years in force. Third, they did not reflect a lack of interest in land: the CHP withdrew from the 1973 law in the period in which land reform occupied a larger share of parliamentary debate than at any time since 1945 (supplement Figure A3). What distinguishes the votes on which the opposition withdrew from those on which it voted no is the content of the measure and the opposition's chance of winning, as the repertoire hypothesis predicts.

Two votes on land transfers fall among the recorded oppositions, and each is predicted by the repertoire hypothesis. The first is the only land-transfer vote on which the opposition was pivotal. In February 1965, the CHP-led coalition moved that İnönü's land reform bill be considered by a temporary committee drawn from seven standing committees (mm01035055, p.~50). The AP turned out at 1.65 times its usual rate (index $+0.50$), voted no almost unanimously, and defeated the motion by 167 to 157; the bill never reached a final vote. The second is the 1966 titling law, which the CHP opposed openly at its usual level of attendance (point 4).\footnote{The bill first failed for want of a quorum on May 5, 1966, when only 39 deputies took part (mm02006092, p.~86); it passed the next day by 227 to 77 (mm02006093, p.~21). TİP's amendments limiting registration to 100 dönüm of dry and 20 of irrigated land were rejected by a show of hands (mm02006092, pp.~50--55).} This was the first land bill after the CHP adopted its ``left of center'' program, and the CHP and TİP presented the titling of untitled land to its possessors as a measure favoring large holders. For a party that had articulated land as part of its program, the recorded position was the point---the one case in the record where the articulated land hypothesis predicts behavior that the other accounts do not.

Where, then, did the opponents of land transfers fight when they did not vote? The amendment of the 1945 law in March 1950 answers this. Two months before the elections that would end its rule, the CHP itself diluted its own law. The minister who presented the amendment was Cavit Oral, who in 1945 had been the most persistent critic of Article 17 (08025063, pp.~37--39). A CHP deputy reminded the assembly that at the party's own congress the delegates had ``all at once attacked articles 15, 16, 17 and 43'' (08025063, p.~20), and another, a landowner, complained that sharecropping arrangements had ended ``all from the fear of Article 17'' (p.~37). The fifteen deputies who voted against the dilution sat on the CHP's benches or with it, among them Şevket Raşit Hatipoğlu, the agriculture minister who had carried the law in 1945 and who now called it ``the only legacy of honor I can leave my children'' (p.~25). The amendment passed by 261 to 15 (08025070, p.~87). The landed critics of 1945 had not been defeated by a vote. They had used voice inside the governing party, at its congress and in its committees, and had rewritten the law from within, with one of them in the cabinet.

Exit completed the repertoire. Twice in the period, a group of deputies whose interests in land diverged from their party's left the governing party: the future DP founders in 1945--46, who had criticized the land law and broken with the cabinet on its budget, and the future founders of the Demokratik Parti in 1970. The latter had broken with their party on a single agrarian issue before they left: on the 1970 property tax, which extended taxation to agricultural land, a third of them voted no, against 1 percent of the deputies who stayed in the AP (Fisher's exact $p=5\times10^{-9}$). This is the one place in the record where a deputy's own agrarian position anticipated his partisan behavior. It concerned the taxation of land rather than its distribution, it was expressed as a recorded vote of the kind the repertoire hypothesis predicts for taxes, and it was followed by exit.

\subsection*{The Conditions of Redistribution}

Land reform reached a recorded vote at five moments in the period and passed at two: in June 1945, by 345 to 0, and in June 1973, by 238 to 5 (supplement Table A4, Panel A). The landed veto hypothesis cannot account for this pattern, because landed deputies never cast the recorded votes against reform that a veto would require; the only recorded defeat of a reform measure, the 1965 motion, was inflicted by the opposition party as a whole, whose farmer deputies voted exactly like its other deputies. Nor can the radical flank hypothesis. TİP was pivotal on none of the 78 agrarian roll calls of the 1965--69 Assembly, took part in only 37 percent of them, and saw its ceiling amendments rejected without a recorded vote; reform came after it had been closed (supplement Table A4, Panel B).

What the two passages share is the absence of a party to which the opponents of reform could exit. In 1945 the regime was a single party; the law passed in the narrow window between the announcement of competitive politics and its arrival, when the critics of the law could speak and withdraw but could not yet take their votes to another party. Within months they could---the DP was founded in January 1946---and five years later the CHP diluted its own law before the first election it would lose. In 1973 the cabinets were formed under military supervision after the memorandum of 1971, the radical left had been closed, and partisan conflict on agrarian votes had collapsed: the distance between the AP and CHP yes shares fell by 0.38 after the memorandum, a drop larger than at 97 percent of placebo break dates in other periods (supplement Figure A2). The AP, whose deputies had voted 2 to 127 against İnönü's land reform motion in 1965, voted 198 to 1 for the 1973 law, and the CHP stayed away. Under open competition, by contrast, every governing party used land policy for its own coalition: the CHP diluted its law before an election, the DP and the AP passed titling laws that confirmed possessors' claims, and İnönü's bill was killed by a mobilized opposition. Reform passed when exit was not available, and its passage depended on that condition.

\section*{DISCUSSION AND CONCLUSIONS}

I conclude by discussing what these results imply for theories of landed interests: (1) in a state that administered the countryside, landed interests were represented through the struggle over the cabinet rather than through class or constituency voting; (2) landed opposition followed a repertoire in which the recorded vote was used selectively and voice, absence, and exit carried the weight; and (3) redistribution depended less on outvoting landed deputies than on removing their exit option.

\subsection*{Landed Interests and the Cabinet}

I have found no evidence that deputies' own land or their constituencies shaped their positions on agrarian measures. The parties did not differ in their landed composition, landed deputies voted like their party colleagues, and the place a deputy represented added little to his party. Agrarian positions followed cabinet alignment instead, and changed when, and only when, alignment changed. This is not the absence of a social basis of agrarian politics but a particular way of organizing it. In Turkey between 1945 and 1980, the state bought grain, ran farms and forests, built irrigation, and, after 1973, administered land reform itself. The budgets and accounts of these agencies make up most of the agrarian roll calls in the record, and they were precisely the votes on which government and opposition divided. To vote on them was to vote on who would control the resources that landowners and peasants alike depended on. Under these conditions, the most effective way to represent landed interests was not to defend them on the floor but to be in, or close to, the government that allocated them.

This suggests a scope condition for class and constituency accounts of agrarian representation. Where legislation allocates agrarian resources and agrarian interests are organized through markets and private associations, landed and rural deputies have reason to defend those interests with their votes, and landed or agrarian voting blocs should be visible, as they were in parts of northern and central Europe where agrarian parties formed \citep{lipset1967}. Where the executive allocates agrarian resources, as in many late-developing states, landed interests should be represented through the struggle for executive control, and landed voting blocs should be rare even where landed interests are powerful. The comparison with the classic account of cabinet government is instructive. \citet{cox1987} attributed the cohesion of Victorian parties to the cabinet's monopoly over the legislative agenda; in Turkey, the cabinet's monopoly over the agenda was matched by its monopoly over the administration of the countryside, so that the vote on the government was also a vote on the distribution of agrarian resources. The articulation of agrarian conflict as a question of cabinet control was, in this sense, an articulation by the state as much as by parties \citep{deleon2009,shefter1994}.

\subsection*{The Repertoire of Landed Opposition}

The absence of landed voting blocs should not be mistaken for the absence of landed influence. Landed deputies in Turkey spoke far beyond their numbers in the debates that mattered, shaped legislation in committee and at their party's congress, placed one of their own in charge of rewriting the 1945 law, and, when the governing party would not accommodate them, left it. Their choice among forms of opposition was systematic. They recorded their opposition openly to the cabinet's budgets, to taxes on land, and on the one land vote they could win; they withheld their votes, rather than voting no, on measures that transferred land or titles to rural claimants; and they spoke against such measures at length. A party that had made land its program recorded its opposition even on transfers. This pattern is what theories of credit claiming and blame avoidance predict \citep{mayhew1974,weaver1986}: a recorded vote against giving land to peasants is a visible position against numerous constituents, while a recorded vote against a tax on one's own land is not. In \citeauthor{hirschman1970}'s (\citeyear{hirschman1970}) terms, absence adds a third option between voice and exit, one that allows deputies to withhold support without paying the price of open opposition.

This has a methodological implication for the study of interests in legislatures. Analyses that search for the influence of landed or other social interests in roll-call votes can find precise nulls even when those interests are decisive, because the recorded vote is the arena they avoid. Like other informal organization, landed influence leaves its traces in the debates around decisions, in patterns of absence, and in movements between parties; only by reading the vote together with the debate, the absence, and the exit can it be seen. It also has a substantive implication. The cost of a recorded position was structured by the electorate, so that the deputies who held land were constrained by the peasants who did not. Where the vote is a public act before a rural electorate, absence becomes a form of agrarian politics in its own right---a parliamentary counterpart, practiced by the strong, of the everyday forms of resistance through which \citet{scott1985} showed the weak pursue their interests without open confrontation.

\subsection*{Redistribution and the Exit Option}

Finally, these findings suggest a mechanism for the authoritarian timing of land reform \citep{huntington1968,albertus2015}. In Turkey, landed deputies never vetoed reform with their votes. What blocked reform under competition was that the governing party could not afford to alienate a landed wing that could leave it for the opposition, as the DP's founders had left the CHP in 1945--46 and as the Demokratik Parti's founders would leave the AP in 1970. Reform passed when there was nowhere to exit to: under a single party whose dissidents had withdrawn but not yet organized, and under military-supervised cabinets after the radical left had been closed and partisan conflict suspended. This account is consistent with arguments that the organization of conservative interests shapes the possibilities of democratic reform \citep{ziblatt2017}, but it locates the decisive variable not in the presence of landed deputies in parliament but in the availability of a party to which they can defect.

The comparative implications should be stated carefully, because the Turkish record alone cannot establish them. Several of the most extensive land reforms of the twentieth century were carried out under occupation or single-party rule---in Japan, Taiwan, and South Korea after the Second World War, among others---while reforms enacted by competitive legislatures with strong landed representation have often been diluted. The exit option account implies that reform under competitive rule should be most likely where the main parties converge on reform, where the opposition is itself committed to redistribution, or where the costs of switching parties are high, and that reform enacted when exit is blocked should be vulnerable when competition returns. The Turkish case is consistent with the last implication: the 1973 law was annulled by the Constitutional Court in 1977, after competition had returned.\footnote{The annulment left no recorded vote and is treated here as historical context rather than as evidence from the record.}

My evidence has limits that bear on these generalizations. Most agrarian policy in Turkey was decided by decree or by a show of hands, and the record covers the named votes, not policy as a whole; it says nothing about the effects of these policies on farmers. The occupational measure records whether a deputy farmed or owned land, not how much, so the null results concern deputies with a stake in land rather than the very largest landowners. The Senate is excluded because senators' parties are not observed. The repertoire analysis rests on nine key votes, and the claim about redistribution on two passages, so both describe patterns consistent with the mechanisms rather than identified effects. The within-party and within-deputy designs, by contrast, identify the relevant comparisons under clearly stated assumptions and replicate across two party systems, two electoral systems, and three decades.

The Turkish parliament between 1945 and 1980 was a place where land mattered. It passed two land reform laws, diluted one, and buried another, and its debates on land were among the most heated in its history. Yet the deputies who owned land, and those who represented the countryside, never formed a bloc in the record of its votes. They voted with the cabinet when their party held it and against it when it did not, and they defended their land in the places the vote left open: the debate, the party congress, the empty seat, and the door to another party. To see how landed interests act in a democracy, one must look not only at how deputies vote but at when they speak, when they stay away, and when they leave.
