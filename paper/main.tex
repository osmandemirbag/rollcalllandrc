\begin{abstract}
\noindent Theories of agrarian politics expect land to divide legislatures: landed elites should defend property, rural deputies should defend the countryside, and land reform should pit classes or regions against one another. Using a novel record of every named vote on land and agriculture in the Turkish parliament from 1945 to 1980, linked to the biographies of the deputies who cast them and to the debates on the decisive land laws, I show that this is not how the countryside entered the legislature. Agrarian roll calls were organized by a single division between the government and the opposition. The same deputies reversed their votes on the same farm agencies when their party entered or left the cabinet; deputies who left the governing party turned against its farm budgets, while those who moved between opposition parties did not. Deputies who owned or worked land, and those who represented rural provinces, voted like their party colleagues in every period. Landed interests acted instead through voice in debate, absence from the vote, and exit from the party, and land was redistributed only when there was no competing party to exit to. I argue that agrarian conflict was articulated as a struggle over control of the cabinet that administered the countryside. The findings suggest that in strong states the articulation of social cleavages is an integral part of the contest for the state.

\medskip
\noindent\textbf{Keywords:} political articulation, agrarian politics, land reform, roll calls, state formation, Turkey
\end{abstract}

Although the agrarian question was central to classical political sociology, the study of how it was fought out inside representative institutions has largely been left to others. Comparative-historical sociology made the relation between landlords, peasants, and the state the hinge of its accounts of democracy and dictatorship \citep{moore1966,paige1975,skocpol1979}, and the theory of party systems placed the conflict between land and industry among the critical cleavages that national revolutions bequeathed to modern parties \citep{lipset1967}. Yet when sociologists turned to parties themselves, they largely ceded the analysis of legislative behavior to political scientists \citep{mudge2014}. Recent work has begun to reclaim the party as a sociological object, showing that parties do not merely translate social divisions into politics but actively constitute them \citep{desai2002,deleon2009,deleon2015,eidlin2016}, and that the formation of parties is bound up with the formation of the state \citep{rohr2025}. What this work has not yet examined is how the most classic of substantive cleavages, the conflict over land, was articulated in the place where it was ultimately decided: the vote.

Sociologists and political scientists continue to approach this question with different expectations. Sociologists typically treat agrarian conflict as \textit{exogenous} to politics, rooted in the class structure of the countryside, in the opposition between rural and urban sectors, or in the relation between a central state and its periphery \citep{moore1966,bates1981,mardin1973}. On this view, landed interests should appear in parliament as a recognizable bloc: deputies who own land, or who represent the countryside, should vote against redistribution and land taxation, and the conflict over land should be visible as a dimension of legislative voting in its own right. Political scientists, by contrast, have increasingly treated legislative parties as \textit{endogenous} to the problems of office holders, and in parliamentary systems the central problem is the survival of the cabinet \citep{cox1987,huber1996,cox2005}. On this view, what deputies vote on matters less than who controls the government that proposed it. Both approaches could have validity, but for different settings, and the specification of the conditions under which one or the other holds is precisely what \citet[322]{mudge2014} call for in order ``to avoid false universals.''

Turkey offers an unusually strategic case for this specification. Its competitive party system was born in a dispute over land. In 1945 the single-party government of the Republican People's Party (CHP) introduced a law to distribute land to farmers, including by expropriating land worked by sharecroppers; among its most prominent critics were the deputies who, a few months later, founded the Democrat Party (DP), which would end single-party rule in 1950. The received account accordingly treats the land law as the moment when landed notables broke with the single party and carried their interests into the new party system \citep{karpat1959,keyderpamuk1984}. Land reform returned to the agenda in every decade that followed, under a two-party system, a fragmented multiparty system with a radical left, and a period of military tutelage, and it passed again only once, in 1973. If landed interests organized legislative conflict anywhere, they should have done so here. Yet systematic evidence on how deputies actually voted on land has been lacking, because the record of parliamentary votes for this period has never been assembled.

Using a novel record of every named vote on land and agriculture in the Turkish Grand National Assembly from 1945 to 1980---511 roll calls and 216,315 individual votes, linked to the biographies of 2,979 deputies and to the speeches given in the decisive land debates---I show that, contrary to the cleavage accounts, landed interests never formed a voting bloc. Agrarian roll calls were organized by a single division between the government and the opposition, in every period in which there was an opposition. The same deputies reversed their votes on the same farm agencies when their party entered or left the cabinet. Deputies who left the governing party turned against its farm budgets, while deputies who moved from one opposition party to another did not change their votes at all. Deputies who owned or worked land, and those who represented rural provinces, voted exactly like their party colleagues. Landed deputies were not silent: they dominated the debates on land and shaped legislation from inside their parties. But their opposition took three forms---voice in debate, absence from the vote, and exit from the governing party---and never the form of a recorded vote against their own side. And land was redistributed only when there was no competing party for its opponents to exit to.

I argue that these regularities reflect what I call \emph{cabinet articulation}. In a state that administered the countryside through executive agencies---a grain board, state farms, an irrigation authority, a land-reform administration---every vote on agriculture was also a vote on the government that ran them, and parties articulated agrarian conflict as a struggle over control of the cabinet rather than as a division among deputies. This argument extends the state-centered account of party formation proposed by \citet{rohr2025} from the formal questions on which they found the first American parties united to the substantive questions that sociologists have regarded as the very stuff of cleavage. It also suggests a mechanism for one of the most robust findings on land reform, namely that redistribution is more common under authoritarian rule than under democracy \citep{huntington1968,albertus2015}. If landed interests in competitive parliaments act through voice, absence, and exit rather than through votes, reform requires not a defeat of landed deputies on the floor but the suspension of the party competition that gives exit its force.

\section*{THEORIES OF AGRARIAN CONFLICT IN PARLIAMENTS}

\subsection*{Sociology on Land and Cleavage}

The sociological study of agrarian politics begins from the structure of rural society. In the comparative-historical tradition, the decisive variables are the strength of landed classes, the mode in which they extract labor from the peasantry, and the alliances they form with the bourgeoisie and the state. \citet{moore1966} argued that the survival of a labor-repressive landed class was the principal obstacle to democracy; \citet{paige1975} tied the forms of rural conflict to the ways in which cultivators and noncultivators derive their income from land; and a long line of work has followed in treating the political behavior of landlords as a direct expression of their position in agrarian class structure. If this view holds in parliamentary politics, deputies who own or work land should defend it, and the conflict over land should divide them from other deputies regardless of party---what I call the \textit{landed class cleavage} hypothesis. Its strongest implication is that landed deputies should vote against redistribution and land taxation even when their party supports them, and that the division between landed and other deputies should be visible in the structure of voting.

The theory of party systems offers a related but more general expectation. \citet{lipset1967} famously argued that European party systems were the product of a small number of critical cleavages, among them the conflict between landed and industrial interests, and that the degree to which these cleavages were institutionalized determined the shape of party competition. Where the conflict between land and industry was strong, it gave rise to agrarian parties; where it was not, it was absorbed into the programs of broader parties. In either case, the logic of party formation is driven by the substantive organization of the polity, and parties should be distinguishable by the social composition of their members and by their positions on the issues that define the cleavage. In Turkey, where no agrarian party of significance formed, the cleavage hypothesis implies that the main parties should differ in their relation to land: the CHP, as the party of the bureaucratic center, should be less rooted in landed society than the DP and its successors, and their deputies should differ accordingly.

A second, spatial version of the cleavage argument locates agrarian conflict not in deputies' class positions but in their constituencies. Theories of urban bias hold that agricultural policy in developing countries reflects the political weakness of a dispersed rural majority against concentrated urban interests \citep{bates1981}, and theories of collective action imply that rural interests will be represented to the extent that rural representatives can coordinate \citep{olson1965}. In the Turkish literature, the most influential version of this argument is \citeauthor{mardin1973}'s (\citeyear{mardin1973}) account of a confrontation between a centralizing bureaucratic elite and a provincial periphery, in which the DP and its successors gave political voice to provincial notables and peasants against the Kemalist state. Both arguments imply what I call the \textit{rural constituency} hypothesis: deputies from rural provinces, or from provinces in agrarian distress, should form a bloc on agrarian votes, and a rural or regional dimension should appear in the structure of legislative voting. A strong version of the center--periphery account, which I call the \textit{regional cleavage} hypothesis, would further imply that the place from which a deputy was elected predicts his vote better than his party.

Both versions of the cleavage argument have been criticized for treating social divisions as latent in the polity, awaiting only a political entrepreneur to activate them. Even so, they share a clear empirical expectation. Whether the relevant division is between landed and other deputies, or between rural and urban constituencies, it should be visible in the votes themselves, and it should survive the comparison of deputies within the same party on the same roll call.

\subsection*{Articulation and the Radical Flank}

The articulation approach rejects the assumption that parties reflect preexisting social divisions. Parties, on this view, ``naturalize'' some divisions and suppress others, building political blocs out of heterogeneous social materials and constituting the very cleavages that cleavage theories take as given \citep{deleon2009,deleon2015}. The approach was developed in part on the Turkish case, where \citet{deleon2009} and \citet{tugal2009} show how parties articulated a division between religious and secular constituencies that did not follow mechanically from social structure. Articulation theory also recognizes that parties may work to keep divisions off the political agenda, and that the suppression of a cleavage can be as consequential as its activation \citep{eidlin2016}. For agrarian politics, this approach implies that land becomes a partisan issue to the extent that parties make it one---what I call the \textit{articulated cleavage} hypothesis. Its indicators are that land votes should divide parties when party leaders politicize land, and only then, and that deputies should follow their parties' articulation of the issue rather than their own social positions.

Articulation theory leaves room for a further mechanism. A party that articulates a cleavage more radically than its rivals may change outcomes directly, by supplying decisive votes, or indirectly, by pulling more moderate parties toward its position or by provoking concessions designed to undercut it. In the study of social movements, this possibility is known as the radical flank effect \citep{haines1984}. In Turkey, the Workers' Party of Turkey (TİP), which entered parliament in 1965, demanded ceilings on landholding and the distribution of large estates, and its presence coincided with the most intense period of parliamentary debate on land reform. The \textit{radical flank} hypothesis implies that TİP should have been decisive on agrarian votes, or that the parties closest to it should have moved toward its positions.

\subsection*{Political Science on the Cabinet}

Political scientists have tended to treat parties as endogenous organizations formed by office holders to solve their own problems \citep{cox2005}. In parliamentary systems, the defining problem is the survival of the government. \citet{cox1987} showed that cohesive party voting in the British House of Commons developed as the cabinet came to monopolize the legislative agenda and as votes on government measures became, in effect, votes on the government itself. \citet{huber1996} showed how executives use procedural instruments, among them the question of confidence, to turn policy votes into votes on their own survival. On this view, deputies vote with or against the government according to whether their party holds office, and the content of a measure matters primarily through its consequences for the cabinet. I call this the \textit{cabinet} hypothesis. Its indicators are that votes should follow the cabinet status of the deputy's party, that a deputy's vote should change when that status changes, and that the dominant dimension of voting should separate government from opposition rather than one social group from another.

The cabinet hypothesis has a distributive corollary. Measures that distribute benefits to large and dispersed constituencies, such as seed loans, debt relief, and village works, should pass with near-unanimity, since no deputy gains from opposing them on the record, and governments should ration such benefits by controlling the agenda rather than by winning contested votes \citep{weingast1979}. In agrarian politics, this implies that the most contested votes will be not the votes that distribute benefits to the countryside but the votes that fund the agencies through which the government administers it.

\subsection*{Land Reform, Regimes, and History}

A final set of arguments concerns the conditions under which land is redistributed. \citet{huntington1968} argued that land reform is a characteristic project of modernizing regimes insulated from the pressures of landed elites, and \citet{albertus2015} has shown systematically that land reform is far more common under autocracy than under democracy. In Albertus's account, democratic institutions provide landed elites with veto points, while autocracies whose ruling coalition has split from landed elites face no such constraint. In parliamentary terms, this implies what I call the \textit{landed veto} hypothesis: in competitive legislatures, landed deputies should vote down redistribution, and reform should pass only when they are outvoted or excluded.

Historical specificity is especially important here. As \citet{mudge2014} emphasize, the nature of parties changes over time, and in the early phases of a party system politics may be organized not by parties at all but by networks of notables. In the Turkish countryside, the power of large landowners (\textit{ağa}) and provincial notables was widely held to be personal rather than partisan, exercised through families and clienteles that could attach themselves to any party \citep{karpat1959,keyder1987}. If this were the organizing principle of agrarian politics in parliament, voting on land should be organized by \textit{notable clusters}---groups of deputies from the same province or family network who vote together across party lines---rather than by parties. A related, top-down variant holds that in a strong state, agrarian policy was decided by the bureaucracy and the military, and parliament merely ratified it; on this \textit{bureaucratic tutelage} view, partisan conflict over agriculture should be weak throughout, and strongest where the bureaucracy's control was weakest.

\subsection*{The State and Cabinet Articulation}

These theories are usually treated as rivals. I propose instead that they describe different aspects of a single process, and that what connects them is the state. \citet{rohr2025} argue that the first American party system formed not around social cleavages but around the organizational affordances of the modern state: parties coalesced around the structural questions that determined who would fill elected and appointed positions in a growing administration, and they did so because those questions shaped the outcomes of all future contests. Their argument concerns formal issues. Mine concerns substantive ones. In states that administer the countryside through executive agencies---grain boards, state farms, irrigation authorities, land-reform administrations---the substance of agrarian policy is inseparable from control of the government that runs those agencies \citep{mann1984,heper1985}. Under these conditions, parties have strong incentives to articulate agrarian conflict as a question of cabinet control, and deputies have strong incentives to follow, since every vote on agriculture is also a vote on the government.

Landed interests do not disappear in this account. They move to the places where the vote on the cabinet does not constrain them. \citet{hirschman1970} distinguished between voice, the attempt to change an organization from within, and exit, the decision to leave it. In a parliament where the vote is a vote on the cabinet, landed deputies can use voice in debate and in the party's internal forums, can withhold their votes by staying away, and can exit to another party when the stakes are high enough. What they cannot easily do is record a vote against their own party on the floor, especially a vote against the distribution of land to peasants, which is visible to rural electorates. This yields a set of indicators that distinguish \textit{cabinet articulation} from the alternatives: agrarian votes should follow cabinet status within deputies, across regimes and governing parties; party exits should change votes only when they move deputies across the line between government and opposition; deputies' land, occupation, and constituency should not predict their votes within party, though landed deputies may speak more than others; opponents of land distribution should prefer absence to a recorded no, while opponents of land taxation, which imposes costs on themselves rather than benefits on others, may vote no openly; and redistribution should pass only when party competition is absent or suspended, since only then does exit lose its force. \section*{THE CASE: TURKEY, 1945--1980}

I examine agrarian voting in the Turkish parliament from the passage of the 1945 land law to the military coup of 1980. Turkey entered this period as a single-party republic in which the CHP governed a predominantly agrarian society through a strong bureaucratic state \citep{heper1985,keyder1987}. In 1945, as the regime prepared a controlled opening to competitive politics, the government introduced the Law on Providing Land to Farmers (\textit{Çiftçiyi Topraklandırma Kanunu}, Law 4753), whose seventeenth article allowed the state to expropriate land worked by sharecroppers and tenants down to small holdings. During the debate, Celâl Bayar, Adnan Menderes, Fuat Köprülü, and Refik Koraltan, all CHP deputies, submitted a memorandum demanding political liberalization; in January 1946 they founded the DP. The standard account treats the land law as the issue on which landed notables broke with the single party, and Menderes, a landowner from the Aegean, as their spokesman \citep{karpat1959,keyderpamuk1984,zurcher2004}.

The DP won the elections of 1950 and governed for a decade, during which agricultural mechanization, rising crop prices, and an expansion of state credit transformed the countryside and tied the peasantry to the governing party \citep{keyder1987,ahmad1977}. The DP split twice: in 1948, while still in opposition, when dissidents left to found the Millet Partisi (MP), and in 1955, while in government, when dissidents expelled after a dispute over press freedom founded the Hürriyet Partisi (HP). Until 1960, deputies were elected by plurality on provincial party lists, so that each province's delegation usually belonged to a single party. The military coup of 1960 ended DP rule. The constitution of 1961 introduced proportional representation and a second chamber, the Senate of the Republic, and the Justice Party (AP), heir to the DP, became the dominant party of the right.

Land reform returned to the agenda throughout the 1960s and 1970s. The CHP-led coalition of İsmet İnönü introduced a land reform bill in 1965; the bill never reached the floor. The Workers' Party of Turkey (TİP), which entered parliament in the elections of the same year with 15 seats, demanded ceilings on landholding. The CHP's turn toward the ``left of center'' under Bülent Ecevit led its right wing to leave in 1967 and found the Güven Partisi (GP). In 1970, a group of AP deputies voted against their own government's budget and later founded the Demokratik Parti. After the military memorandum of March 12, 1971, cabinets were formed under military supervision, TİP was closed by the Constitutional Court, and in 1973 parliament passed the Land and Agrarian Reform Law (Law 1757) with the votes of the AP. The competitive coalitions that followed---the CHP with the National Salvation Party (MSP) in 1974, the AP-led Nationalist Front from 1975---ended with the coup of 1980.

Three features make this case useful for adjudicating theories of agrarian politics. First, land was salient and recurrent, so a landed cleavage had repeated opportunities to appear. Second, the period offers within-case variation in each of the factors the theories emphasize: the regime (single party, competitive, tutelary), the governing party (CHP, DP, AP, and coalitions), the electoral system (plurality and proportional representation), and the presence of a radical flank. Third, the same kind of record exists throughout. The Turkish parliament recorded named votes on the final passage of laws and on budgets in every period, and from 1961 also on articles and motions, so that the behavior of deputies can be compared across very different political conditions.

That record has nevertheless been largely ignored, for reasons that are instructive. Historians of Turkish politics have relied on memoirs, party documents, and the press to reconstruct the agrarian question, and these sources are biased in a particular way. The power of landed notables was exercised largely through informal channels---family networks, local party organizations, committee rooms, and the corridors of the Assembly---that leave few traces. Informal organization of this kind is easy to overlook and easy to overstate, since a single dramatic speech or memoir may be taken to stand for the behavior of many. Most agrarian policy, moreover, was made by decree or decided by a show of hands, which leaves no individual record at all. The named vote is the one place where the position of every deputy on a given question was publicly recorded. It cannot reveal everything about agrarian politics, and I do not claim that it does. But because the various hypotheses discussed above have clear implications for the patterning of named votes (Table~\ref{tab:hyp}, last column), the record allows them to be tested systematically and in the same way across the whole period.

\section*{DATA AND METHODS}

\subsection*{Data}

I examine every named vote on land and agriculture in the single-chamber Grand National Assembly (TBMM) from April 1945 to the coup of 1960, and in the National Assembly (\textit{Millet Meclisi}) from 1961 to 1980. The analyses draw on three kinds of data: the votes themselves, individual-level information on the deputies who cast them, and the text of the debates on the land laws. Further information on sources, coding, and accuracy is provided in Part A of the online supplement.

\textit{Roll call votes.} The official minutes (\textit{Tutanak Dergisi}) print, for each named vote, the tallies and the full lists of deputies voting yes, no, and abstaining, of those not taking part, and of vacant seats, ordered by province. For 1961--80 I identified all named votes whose subject was land, agricultural credit and prices, commodity boards, forests, irrigation, state farms, or the budgets and accounts of the agencies responsible for them. For 1943--60 I located every printed vote result in a page-level text corpus of the minutes, which yielded 1,872 named votes in five terms, of which 143 concern agriculture (1946--60) and 57 fall in the months around the 1945 land law. I then read the lists from the original page images. Because the format of the lists changed over the period---the 1945 lists print initials and surnames in capitals, and the vocabulary of the sections changed from \textit{Kabul etmiyenler} and \textit{Oya katılmıyanlar} to \textit{Reddedenler} and \textit{Reye iştirak etmiyenler}---each era required its own reading, and every list was reconciled against the printed tallies. A vote is used when no section of its list holds more names than its tally; names lost to unreadable print leave a deputy's choice missing rather than miscoded. Table~\ref{tab:terms} reports the number of deputies and roll calls in each term. The record contains 511 roll calls and 216,315 deputy votes; 178 roll calls were contested, in the sense that the losing side received at least 5 percent of the votes cast.\footnote{The Senate's 245 agrarian votes for 1961--80 were collected in the same way but are not analyzed here, because the party affiliation of senators is not recorded in the available biographical data.}

\textit{Deputies.} Deputies were linked to the Turkish Political Elites Dataset, which records every member of parliament by term, province, and party, together with the characteristics of the province. Matching proceeded within term and province, by surname and initials for 1945 and by full name thereafter; 85 percent of the names on the 1945 lists and 89.5 percent of those on the 1946--60 lists were matched, and the 73 land votes of 1961--80 were verified by hand. To measure deputies' relation to land, I coded the biographies printed in the parliament's official album, the \textit{TBMM Albümü 1920--2010}, which records for every deputy the place and year of birth, education, languages, occupation, and career. The 7,355 biographies for 1920--80 were matched to the elites dataset at rates between 97 and 100 percent in every term. I classify a deputy as a farmer or landowner when the biography lists farming, a farm, land, or livestock as an occupation; the share ranges from 9 percent of deputies in the 1943 Assembly to 18 percent in the 1950s. Adnan Menderes, for example, is listed as engaged in \textit{çiftçilik} (farming), and Emin Sazak, a large landowner from Eskişehir, as a \textit{ziraatçı} (agriculturalist). The biographies do not record the size of holdings, so this measure identifies deputies with a direct stake in land rather than the largest landowners alone.\footnote{Schooling in agriculture is coded separately and not counted. Other occupational categories (merchant, lawyer or judge, military officer, religious office) are coded from the same biographies with the same procedure; see supplement Part A.} I also use deputies' age, education, and birthplace from the elites dataset, the rural share of their province's population, and, for 1968--80, provincial harvest shocks, measured as the deviation of crop yields from each province's trend.

\textit{Debates.} For the debates on the 1945 land law (May 14--21 and June 4--11, 1945) and on its amendment in March 1950, I divided the minutes into speaker turns, using the printed speaker headings, and matched speakers to the roster; 87 percent of the 445 turns in the 1945 debate were matched. The text corpus from which these turns were taken contains occasional errors of character recognition, so every quotation used below was verified against the original page.

\subsection*{Methods}

With these data I conduct four types of analysis. First, to describe the structure of voting, I estimate the positions of deputies in a two-dimensional space for each term of the National Assembly using a Bayesian item-response model, the standard approach to the analysis of legislative voting \citep{poole1997,clinton2004}, fitted to the roll calls whose lists match their tallies. Each deputy is placed so that deputies who vote alike are close together, and the dimensions can then be related to party and to deputies' characteristics. I complement this with two summaries that can be computed for every period, including those without enough contested votes to estimate a spatial model. The \textit{government premium} is the mean, over contested roll calls, of the difference between the yes shares of government and opposition deputies. The variance decomposition, following \citet{rohr2025}, apportions the variance of individual vote choices on each contested roll call between party and province.\footnote{Because province dummies explain some variance by chance---there are up to 67 provinces and a few hundred votes per roll call---I report the share of variance explained by province beyond party net of the share explained when province labels are randomly permuted within party. Before 1961, plurality elections on provincial lists meant that province and party were almost perfectly collinear, so the share of variance attributed to province entered first is not informative for that period.}

Second, to examine the relation between agrarian voting and social characteristics, I compare the social composition of the parties and estimate the difference between deputies with and without a given characteristic within the same party on the same roll call, using party-by-roll-call fixed effects and standard errors clustered by deputy. This design compares, for example, a farmer in the AP with other AP deputies voting on the same question, and so isolates the association between land and the vote from the party's position.

Third, to identify the effect of cabinet status, I exploit changes in status within deputies. The 1950 elections, which brought the DP to power, provide the cleanest such change: I compare deputies elected in both 1946 and 1950 who remained in the DP, whose party moved from opposition to government, with those who remained in the CHP, whose party moved the other way, on the same roll calls before and after the change. For 1961--80, when status changed many times through coalition formation, I use the estimator of \citet{dechaisemartin2020}, which compares deputies whose status switches between consecutive periods with those whose status does not, and report a placebo for periods without switches. I also treat the four parliamentary splits of the period as a natural comparison: two exits from the governing party (1955 and 1970) and two exits between opposition parties (1948 and 1967). For each, the estimand is the difference-in-differences in yes votes between leavers and stayers, before and after the split, with inference by Fisher randomization over which deputies are labelled as leavers \citep{imbens2015}.

Fourth, to trace how the opponents of land laws acted, I compare the attendance of the party that opposed each key land bill with that party's own attendance on the other agrarian votes of the same period, and examine the debates and the sequence of votes on the land laws of 1945, 1950, and 1973 through process tracing \citep{collier2011}, comparing the moments at which land reform reached a vote as controlled cases within a single polity \citep{slater2013}. I refer to votes by the minutes file and page on which the list appears.

\section*{ANALYSES}

\subsection*{Parties and Voting Blocs}

I begin with the most fundamental question: how was agrarian voting organized? If land formed a cleavage of its own, or if voting were organized by networks of notables, the structure of voting should show more than one dimension, and positions on the additional dimensions should be associated with deputies' land, constituencies, or provinces. Figure~\ref{fig:maps} presents the estimated positions of deputies in the two National Assemblies in which the most consequential splits occurred, 1965--69 and 1969--73. In both, the first dimension separates the governing party from the opposition, and the model classifies 97 to 98 percent of individual votes correctly. The AP, the governing party in both terms, forms a tight cluster; the opposition parties are spread out along both dimensions. There is no evidence of many small islands of agreement that would indicate voting organized around notables, and the second dimension is unrelated to the characteristics that cleavage theories emphasize: its within-party correlations with the rural share of a deputy's province and with an eastern constituency are at most 0.10 in absolute value in every term.

In 1965--69, the CHP deputies who left in 1967 to found the Güven Partisi, a split from one opposition party to another, are indistinguishable from the rest of the CHP on the first dimension (mean $-0.69$ against $-0.64$). Because positions are estimated over the whole term, this distance reflects the votes these deputies cast after they left; the analyses of party exits below identify that change directly. Already, however, the maps suggest that what moved deputies in the space of agrarian voting was not their place in society but their place relative to the cabinet.

The intensity of partisan conflict over agriculture, by contrast, changed markedly. No agrarian roll call was contested in 1945, when the CHP was the only party; under the two-party system, 10 to 14 percent were contested in each of the first three terms and 12 of 15 in the polarized parliament of 1957--60; in the fragmented National Assemblies of the 1960s and 1970s, 40 to 75 percent (Table~\ref{tab:terms}). Partisan conflict over agriculture thus grew with each step of competition and receded under military tutelage, contrary to the \textit{bureaucratic tutelage} hypothesis, which expects weak conflict throughout. The organization of agrarian voting, however, did not change: in every period with an opposition, it took the form of two blocs divided by their relation to the cabinet.

\subsection*{Parties and Social Cleavages}

One might imagine that such coordination arose without any particular effort because of a fundamental mapping of the party structure onto existing social cleavages. Where parties recruit from different social strata, they may vote coherently simply because their members share interests. If the DP and the AP were the parties of the landed periphery and the CHP the party of the bureaucratic center, as the cleavage and center--periphery accounts suggest, their deputies should differ in their relation to land, and these differences should account for their votes.

They did not differ in this way. Table~\ref{tab:social} compares the social characteristics of deputies by party. In the 1946--60 parliaments, 18.5 percent of CHP deputies were farmers or landowners, compared with 15.3 percent of DP deputies; in 1961--80, the shares were 14.5 percent for the CHP and 14.1 percent for the AP. The parties were broadly similar, too, in the shares of merchants and lawyers, in the share of deputies born in the province they represented, and in the rural character of their provinces. The differences that do appear are not those the cleavage theories predict: the CHP of the single-party period drew more heavily on military officers and military education, reflecting the bureaucratic origins of the Republic, and this difference declined over time in both parties. Rather than supporting the \textit{landed class cleavage} hypothesis, the composition of the parties suggests that both were built on cross-cutting alliances that included substantial numbers of landed deputies.

The more demanding test compares deputies within the same party on the same roll call (Table~\ref{tab:social}, Panel B). If landed deputies defended their interests on the floor, they should vote differently from their party colleagues on agrarian questions even if the parties as a whole were similar. They did not. On contested agrarian roll calls in 1946--60, farmer and landowner deputies differed from their party colleagues by $-0.002$ (SE 0.007); on contested land and land-tax votes in 1961--80 the difference is $+0.002$ (SE 0.009), and on contested agency budgets $+0.010$ (SE 0.008). Estimated separately for each period, the difference is within two standard errors of zero in all six periods with contested votes (supplement Table A4). The same is true of constituencies: a one standard deviation increase in the rural share of a deputy's province changes the probability of a yes vote by less than half a percentage point in either period, and deputies whose provinces suffered a harvest shock were no more likely to turn out for farm benefits. These are precise estimates, based on hundreds of farmer deputies and tens of thousands of votes, and they leave no room for a landed or rural voting bloc of any substantive size. The only behavioral difference is that farmer deputies took part somewhat less often in votes on land ($-0.030$, SE 0.015)---a hint, to which I return, that landed deputies' disagreement took the form of absence rather than opposition.

Landed deputies were not, however, indifferent to land. In the 1945 debate on the land law, the 41 farmer and landowner deputies made up 9 percent of the house but accounted for 16 percent of the speakers and 23 percent of the words spoken (Table~\ref{tab:social}, Panel B). The 14 deputies who would later sit for the DP, 3 percent of the house, accounted for 16 percent of the words. What they said left no doubt about their position. Menderes told the assembly that the bill's scheme for indivisible family farms was taken ``almost verbatim'' from the National Socialist hereditary farm law (07017055, p.~22). Cavit Oral, a landowner from Seyhan (Adana) who headed the province's farmers' union, protested that ``the provisions of Article 17 recognize no limit in land'' (07018067, p.~32). Emin Sazak was more direct still: ``If I denied that my tie to land affects my speech and my feelings, I would be lying'' (07018067, p.~46). Yet the law passed by 345 votes to none. Not a single deputy recorded a vote against it. Landed interests were articulated in the debate and absent from the vote, a pattern that, as I show below, recurs throughout the period.

\subsection*{What Was Partisan}

If agrarian voting was organized by the cabinet rather than by social cleavages, the votes that divided the parties should be the cabinet's instruments, not the transfer of land or benefits to the countryside. Following \citet{rohr2025}, who showed that the most partisan votes in the early New York Assembly concerned the structural rules of the game rather than the substantive issues usually thought to divide the parties, I examine which kinds of agrarian roll calls were contested. Of the 457 agrarian roll calls taken between 1946 and 1980, 178 were contested (supplement Table A1). Their composition is lopsided. Budgets and final accounts of the agrarian agencies make up 64 percent of all agrarian roll calls but 84 percent of the contested ones; half of them (149 of 291) were contested, and every one of the eight budgets of the land-reform agency was. All eight votes on the taxation of land and property were contested. By contrast, only 2 of the 14 votes on land distribution and titling, 2 of the 37 votes on farm benefits, 1 of the 22 votes on commodity boards, and none of the 25 international agricultural agreements were contested. What divided the parties was not whether land should be distributed or the countryside supported. It was who would control the agencies that did so, and who would pay for them.

The record of each period bears this out. Under the CHP government of 1946--50, the DP opposition voted against the budgets of the agrarian agencies---the 1949 budget of the Forest Administration passed by 313 to 25 (08016054, p.~191)---and against the government's increase of the consumption tax on sugar, which passed by 219 to 33, with 14 DP and 14 Millet Partisi deputies voting no (08014022, p.~48). Yet no opposition deputy voted against the distribution of seed to needy farmers, which passed by 273 to 0 with the seven DP deputies present voting yes (08012085, p.~103), or against the law establishing the State Farms, which passed by 251 to 1 with ten DP deputies voting yes and two abstaining (08020103, p.~121). Under the DP government, the roles were reversed. The CHP voted against the agencies' budgets, with increasing intensity as the decade wore on: the Forest Administration's budget passed by 182 to 53 in 1951 (09005053, p.~157), and in the polarized parliament elected in 1957 the State Farms' 1959 budget passed by 314 to 96 and the State Hydraulic Works' by 337 to 86 (11007046, p.~175; 11007048, p.~128). Yet not one CHP deputy voted against the law establishing the State Hydraulic Works in 1953 (245 to 1), the reorganization of the Soil Products Office in 1955 (289 to 1), or the Sugar Law of 1956 (275 to 0); those who took part voted yes.\footnote{09026019, p.~42; 10007078, p.~83; 10012080, p.~31. The closest the CHP came to opposing a substantive agrarian law was the Forest Law of 1956, which passed by 261 to 13; seven of the eight CHP deputies who took part, among them İnönü, voted against it (10013103, p.~59).} The agencies themselves were not in dispute. Their budgets, which were votes on the government that ran them, were.

The National Assembly of the 1960s and 1970s shows the same logic in a form that rules out ideology as its source. After 1973, the budget of the land-reform agency became one of the most reliably partisan votes in the record. The National Salvation Party (MSP), which sat in coalition first with the CHP and then with the AP---parties at opposite ends of the land question---voted for that budget in 1974 as the CHP's partner (45 to 0) and in 1976 and 1977 as the AP's (33 to 0 and 37 to 0), and against it in 1978 and 1979, when the CHP governed without it (0 to 11 and 0 to 4).\footnote{Counts are MSP deputies voting yes and no on the budget of the Land and Agrarian Reform Undersecretariat. The MSP also voted yes in 1975, under the caretaker cabinet of Sadi Irmak, and in 1980, when it supported Süleyman Demirel's minority government.} A party that voted for land reform's administration with either partner, and against it when out of the cabinet, was not expressing a position on land. As in early New York, partisanship was concentrated on the questions that determined who would control the state's resources, and those questions included, in a state that administered the countryside directly, the agencies through which it did so.

\subsection*{The Cabinet and the Formation of Agrarian Conflict}

I have found no evidence that the exogenous characteristics of deputies can explain the organization of agrarian voting. That does not in itself demonstrate that the cabinet organized it: parties with different agrarian programs might simply take turns in government, and their deputies might vote according to those programs. To distinguish these possibilities, I turn to changes in cabinet status within deputies (Table~\ref{tab:designs}).

The 1950 elections provide the sharpest test, because the same deputies, who kept their party, voted on the same kinds of agrarian roll calls before and after their party's relation to the cabinet was reversed. The 27 DP deputies elected in both 1946 and 1950 had voted yes on 5 percent of contested agrarian roll calls in opposition; in government, they voted yes on all of them. The 41 CHP deputies elected in both years fell from 99 percent to 40 percent. The difference-in-differences, which adds the two reversals together, is 1.54, and no random reassignment of party labels among these deputies produces a value as large ($p<.001$). These were the same men voting on the budgets of the same agencies---the Forest Administration, the State Farms, the State Hydraulic Works---and their positions reversed with the cabinet. In the National Assembly of 1961--80, where status changed through the formation and collapse of coalitions, the effect of entering government on the probability of a yes vote is 0.41 (95 percent CI 0.11 to 0.50), with a placebo of 0.002 in periods without a change of status; entering government also raised participation by 0.22. The estimate is smaller than in 1950, plausibly because coalition partners and technocratic cabinets blurred what being in government meant, but its direction is the same, across two constitutions, two electoral systems, and three decades.

The four party splits of the period offer a second, independent test, and one that addresses an obvious objection to the first. A deputy whose party enters government might change his votes because his party's program changes, or because he gains access to the benefits the agencies distribute. A party split separates these mechanisms, because it changes a deputy's party without necessarily changing his relation to the cabinet. All four splits were driven by disputes that had little to do with agrarian budgets---press freedom in 1955, the CHP's turn to the left in 1967, the leadership of the AP in 1970---but only two of them moved deputies across the line between government and opposition. Figure~\ref{fig:exits} and Panel B of Table~\ref{tab:designs} show the results. The DP deputies who left the governing party to found the Hürriyet Partisi in 1955 had voted for every agrarian agency budget before the split; afterward they voted for 48 percent of them, while the deputies who stayed in the DP continued to vote for all of them ($-0.52$, $p<.001$). Fifteen years later, under a different constitution and with a different party, the AP deputies who left to found the Demokratik Parti fell from 100 percent to 14 percent, while their former colleagues remained near unanimity ($-0.83$, $p<.001$). The two exits between opposition parties produce nothing of the kind. The DP deputies who left their party for the Millet Partisi in 1948, when both parties were in opposition, changed their votes by $+0.10$ ($p=.23$); the CHP deputies who left their party for the Güven Partisi in 1967, when both were in opposition, changed theirs by $-0.04$ ($p=.46$). Leaving a party did not change how deputies voted on agriculture. Leaving the government did.

One exit preceded its own cause in a revealing way. Before the AP split, the future founders of the Demokratik Parti had already broken with their party on a single issue: the 1970 property tax, which extended taxation to agricultural land. A third of them voted against it, against 1 percent of the deputies who stayed in the AP (Fisher's exact $p=5\times10^{-9}$).\footnote{The tax passed by 136 to 97 (mm03007115, p.~75).} This is the one place in the record where a substantive agrarian position anticipates partisan behavior, and the issue is not the distribution of land but its taxation. The asymmetry is not accidental, and it points to how landed interests acted when they did not act through votes.

\subsection*{Voice, Absence, and Exit}

What, then, did the opponents of land laws do? The 1945 law provides the first answer, and it corrects the received account. On May 29, 1945, two weeks before the vote on the land law, the prime minister made the seven-month budget a question of confidence. Bayar, Menderes, Koraltan, Köprülü, and Sazak cast the only five votes against it (07017065, p.~41). From that day, they stopped voting almost entirely: they were absent from 91 percent of the named votes that followed, among them the land law of June 11 (supplement Figure A1). Measured against their own absence on the other votes of those months, their absence on the land law is not distinctive ($p=.18$ over 39 votes). The founders of the DP did not single out the land law, as the received account holds. They withdrew from the single party's votes in general, after recording their opposition on the one vote that was explicitly a vote on the cabinet, and they returned in December 1945 only to vote against the 1946 budget. Their debate on the land law was voice; their absence from its vote was part of their exit.

The same pattern---voice in debate, absence from the vote, and recorded opposition on the cabinet's budget or on taxes---recurs in every period. Figure~\ref{fig:land} shows, for each key land vote between 1950 and 1973, how the party that opposed the bill behaved, measured as its attendance relative to its own attendance on the other agrarian votes of the same period. On every law that distributed or registered land, the opposing party stayed away. The DP attended the CHP's 1950 dilution of the land law at 0.69 of its usual rate and the CHP's 1950 titling law at 0.38; the CHP attended the DP's 1955 amendment of the land law at 0.28, the AP's 1966 titling amendment at 0.13, and the 1973 land reform law at 0.04, when 2 of its 101 listed deputies took part. On every law that taxed land, by contrast, the opposing party attended at or near its usual rate and voted no: 83 percent of present AP deputies voted against the 1964 land tax, 96 percent of present CHP deputies against the 1970 property tax, and every present Demokratik Parti deputy against its 1972 amendment.

The single exception confirms the logic. In February 1965, the CHP-led coalition moved that İnönü's land reform bill be considered by a temporary committee drawn from seven standing committees (mm01035055, p.~50). This was the only vote on land distribution in the whole period that its opponents could win. The AP turned out at 1.65 times its usual rate, voted no almost unanimously, and defeated the motion by 167 to 157. The bill never reached a final vote. Opponents of redistribution recorded their opposition when it could prevail; otherwise they left the floor to the government. The asymmetry between distribution and taxation follows the same logic. A recorded vote against giving land to peasants is costly with rural electorates; a recorded vote against taxing one's own land is not. And measures that distribute benefits to the countryside without a visible loser---seed loans, debt relief, village water---passed with the near-unanimity that theories of legislative universalism predict \citep{weingast1979}.

Where, then, did the opponents of land distribution fight? The amendment of the 1945 law in March 1950 shows where. Two months before the elections that would end its rule, the CHP itself diluted its own law. The minister who presented the amendment was Cavit Oral, who in 1945 had been the most persistent critic of Article 17 (08025063, pp.~37--39). A CHP deputy reminded the assembly that at the party's own congress the delegates had ``all at once attacked articles 15, 16, 17 and 43'' (08025063, p.~20), and another, a landowner, complained that sharecropping arrangements had ended ``all from the fear of Article 17'' (p.~37). The fifteen deputies who voted against the dilution sat on the CHP's benches or with it, among them Şevket Raşit Hatipoğlu, the agriculture minister who had carried the law in 1945 and who now called it ``the only legacy of honor I can leave my children'' (p.~25). The amendment passed by 261 to 15 (08025070, p.~87); most DP deputies were absent. The landed critics of 1945 had not been defeated by a vote. They had used voice inside the party, at its congress and in its committees, and they had rewritten the law from inside the governing party, with one of them in the cabinet.

\subsection*{When Land Was Redistributed}

Land reform reached a recorded vote at five moments in the period and passed at two: in June 1945, by 345 to 0, and in June 1973, by 238 to 5 (supplement Table A4, Panel A). What the two passages share is not a weakened landed class, a radical flank, or a reformist party in government, but the absence of a competing party against which the cabinet's opponents could vote, or to which they could exit. In 1945 the regime was a single party, and its dissidents had withdrawn from its votes. In 1973 the cabinets were formed under military supervision after the memorandum of 1971, the radical left had been closed, and partisan conflict on the floor had collapsed: the distance between the AP and CHP yes shares on agrarian roll calls fell by 0.38 after the memorandum, a drop larger than at 97 percent of placebo break dates in other periods (supplement Figure A2). The AP, whose deputies had voted 2 to 127 against İnönü's land reform motion in 1965, voted 198 to 1 for the 1973 law.

Under open competition, by contrast, every governing party used land policy for its own coalition. The CHP diluted its own law before an election; the DP and the AP passed titling laws that confirmed possessors' claims; and İnönü's bill was killed by a mobilized opposition. The radical flank did not change this. TİP, which proposed ceilings of 100 dönüm of dry and 20 of irrigated land during the debate on the 1966 titling law, was pivotal on none of the 78 agrarian roll calls of the 1965--69 Assembly; its ceiling amendments were rejected by a show of hands that left no record (mm02006092, pp.~50--55); its deputies took part in only 37 percent of agrarian votes; and the CHP deputies who left the party in 1967 in reaction to its turn toward the left did not change their votes (supplement Table A4, Panel B). Reform came only after the radical party had been closed and competition suspended. These findings are consistent with the greater frequency of land reform under authoritarian rule \citep{albertus2015}, but the mechanism they reveal differs from a \textit{landed veto}: landed deputies never cast the recorded votes against reform that a veto would require. What the suspension of competition removed was not their votes but their exit option---the possibility of taking their support to another party---that had made every competitive government's agrarian policy hostage to its landed wing.

\section*{DISCUSSION AND CONCLUSIONS}

I conclude by discussing the theoretical purchase afforded by these results: (1) agrarian conflict in the Turkish parliament was articulated through the struggle for control of the cabinet that administered the countryside, rather than through a cleavage among deputies; (2) the absence of agrarian voting blocs does not imply the absence of agrarian power, which was exercised through voice, absence, and exit; and (3) land was redistributed when party competition was absent or suspended, because only then did landed interests lose the exit option on which their influence rested.

\subsection*{Cabinet Articulation and the State}

I have found no evidence that the social characteristics of deputies organized agrarian voting in Turkey. The parties were remarkably similar in their relation to land, landed deputies voted like their party colleagues, and the place a deputy came from added almost nothing to his party. Instead, agrarian voting was organized throughout by deputies' relation to the cabinet, and it changed when, and only when, that relation changed. This is consistent with the articulation approach, in that parties actively shaped which divisions were expressed on the floor \citep{deleon2009}, but the division they articulated was not between social groups. It was between those inside and outside the government.

Why should the most classic of substantive cleavages have been absorbed in this way? The answer, I suggest, lies in the organizational affordances of the state, in the sense developed by \citet{rohr2025}. In New York in the 1790s, parties coalesced around the structural questions that determined who would control appointments to an expanding administration. In Turkey between 1945 and 1980, the state administered the countryside directly. It bought grain through the Soil Products Office, ran state farms and forests, built irrigation through the State Hydraulic Works, and, after 1973, administered land reform itself. The budgets and accounts of these agencies make up most of the agrarian roll calls in my record, and they were precisely the votes on which partisanship was most intense. To vote on them was to vote on the government that controlled them. In such a state, parties had every reason to articulate agrarian conflict as a question of who held the cabinet, and deputies had every reason to follow, since the resources that mattered to their rural constituents flowed through the government rather than through legislation. The countryside entered parliament not as a cleavage but as a stake in the struggle for the state.

This argument suggests a scope condition for cleavage theories of agrarian politics. Where agrarian interests are organized through markets and private associations, and where legislation rather than administration allocates agrarian resources, landed and rural interests may well form voting blocs and agrarian parties, as they did in parts of northern and central Europe \citep{lipset1967}. Where agrarian resources are allocated by the executive, as in many late-developing states, agrarian conflict should instead be articulated through the struggle for executive control, and agrarian voting blocs should be rare even where agrarian interests are powerful. The strength of the state, in other words, shapes not only what parties fight over, as \citet{rohr2025} and \citet{shefter1994} have argued, but also how social cleavages are expressed within the institutions of representation \citep[see also][]{slez2007}.

The comparison with the classic account of cabinet-centered parties is instructive. \citet{cox1987} argued that the cohesion of Victorian parties was the ``efficient secret'' of cabinet government: as the cabinet monopolized the legislative agenda, the private member's vote became a vote on the government, and party discipline followed. The Turkish case adds a sociological twist to this institutional account. The cabinet's monopoly over the agenda was matched by its monopoly over the administration of the countryside, so that the vote on the government was also a vote on the distribution of the resources that rural constituents cared about most. Deputies did not need to be disciplined into voting with their party on agrarian budgets; the agrarian budgets were the party's stake in government. In this sense, cabinet articulation is not the absence of a social basis of partisanship but a particular way of organizing it, in which social interests are bundled into the question of who governs rather than expressed as positions on what the government should do.

It is also worth recalling \citeauthor{shefter1994}'s (\citeyear{shefter1994}) argument that the sequence of bureaucratization and democratization shapes the character of parties: where an autonomous bureaucracy precedes mass politics, parties are expected to compete on programs rather than patronage. Turkey would seem a clear case of the bureaucracy coming first. Yet the agrarian record shows parties competing neither on programs about land nor, primarily, on patronage in appointments, but on control of the agencies through which the bureaucracy reached the countryside. The strong state did not insulate agrarian policy from parties; it concentrated agrarian policy in the one office parties competed for. This suggests that where the state is strong, the line between programmatic and patronage politics may matter less than the line between those who control the executive and those who do not.

\subsection*{Landed Power and the Recorded Vote}

The absence of landed voting blocs should not be mistaken for the absence of landed power. Landed deputies in Turkey spoke far beyond their numbers in the debates that mattered, shaped legislation in committee and at their party's congress, placed one of their own in charge of rewriting the 1945 law, and, when the governing party would not accommodate them, left it. In \citeauthor{hirschman1970}'s (\citeyear{hirschman1970}) terms, they used voice and exit and avoided the one form of opposition that would have made them visible as a bloc. Their choice of instrument was not random. They recorded their opposition openly to the cabinet's budgets and to taxes on their own land, and they withheld their votes, rather than voting no, on laws that distributed land to peasants.

This has a methodological implication for the sociology of parties and legislatures. Analyses that search for social cleavages in roll-call votes can find precise nulls even when the interests in question are decisive, because the recorded vote is the arena those interests avoid. The informal power of notables is, in this respect, like other informal organization: it leaves its traces not in the record of decisions but in the debates around them, in patterns of absence, and in the movement of people between organizations. Only by reading the vote together with the debate and the exit can one see it. The point is not specific to Turkey. Wherever legislative votes are public and party leaders control the agenda, the deputies whose interests diverge from their party's line face a choice between disloyalty, which is visible and costly, and absence, which is neither. That the farmer deputies of 1961--80 voted exactly like their colleagues but took part somewhat less often in votes on land suggests that this choice was made routinely, not only at the dramatic moments of 1945 and 1950. It also has a substantive implication. The distinction between distribution and taxation suggests that the costs of open opposition were themselves structured by the electorate. Where the vote is a public act before a rural electorate, absence becomes a form of agrarian politics in its own right, the parliamentary counterpart of the everyday forms of resistance through which, as \citet{scott1985} showed, the weak pursue their interests without open confrontation---here practiced by the strong.

\subsection*{Redistribution and the Suspension of Competition}

Finally, these findings bear on one of the central questions in the political sociology of land: why redistribution so often comes from authoritarian or tutelary regimes \citep{huntington1968,albertus2015}. In Turkey, landed deputies never vetoed reform with their votes. What blocked reform under competition was that the governing party could not afford to alienate a landed wing that could leave it for the opposition, as the DP's founders had left the CHP in 1945--46 and as the Demokratik Parti's founders would leave the AP in 1970. Reform passed when there was nowhere to exit to: under a single party whose dissidents had withdrawn, and under military-supervised cabinets after the radical left had been closed and partisan conflict suspended. This account is consistent with arguments that the organization of conservative interests shapes the possibilities of democratic reform \citep{ziblatt2017}, and it implies a prediction for other cases: land reform under competitive rule should be most likely where landed interests lack a credible exit option, for example, where the main opposition is itself committed to reform, or where party switching is costly. It also suggests why reform achieved under such conditions may be fragile. The 1973 law was annulled by the Constitutional Court in 1977, after competition had returned; whatever the legal grounds, its survival depended on the same suspension of competition that had made its passage possible.\footnote{The annulment left no recorded vote and is treated here as historical context rather than as evidence from the record.}

The timing of the 1945 law fits this account in a particularly telling way. The law passed in the narrow window between the announcement of competitive politics and its arrival: the single party had already signaled that opposition parties would be permitted, but none yet existed. Its critics could speak, as they did at length, and they could withdraw, as the future DP founders did; what they could not yet do was take their votes and their followers to another party. Within months they could---the DP was founded in January 1946---and five years later the CHP diluted its own law before the first election it would lose. The window in which reform was possible was the window in which exit was not.

The comparative implications of this argument are worth stating carefully, because the Turkish record alone cannot establish them. Several of the most extensive land reforms of the twentieth century were carried out under military occupation or single-party rule---in Japan, Taiwan, and South Korea after the Second World War, among others---while reforms enacted by competitive legislatures with strong landed representation have often been diluted in the legislature or in implementation. \citet{albertus2015} documents the cross-national pattern and attributes it to the veto points that democracy offers landed elites. The Turkish evidence suggests that, at least in parliamentary systems with disciplined parties, the veto may operate less through recorded votes than through the threat of exit, and that the decisive variable is therefore not the presence of landed deputies in parliament but the availability of a party to which they can defect. This yields predictions that can be examined in other cases: reform should be more likely where the main parties converge on reform, where the opposition is itself committed to redistribution, or where the costs of switching parties are high; and reform enacted when exit is blocked should be vulnerable when competition returns.

My evidence has limits that bear on these generalizations. Most agrarian policy in Turkey was decided by decree or by a show of hands, and my record covers the named votes, not policy as a whole; it says nothing about the effects of these policies on farmers. The occupational measure records whether a deputy farmed or owned land, not how much. The Senate is excluded because senators' parties are not observed. And the claim about redistribution rests on two passages, so it describes a pattern consistent with the mechanism rather than an identified effect. The within-deputy and party-exit designs, by contrast, identify the effect of cabinet status under clearly stated assumptions, and they replicate across two party systems, two electoral systems, and three decades.

The Turkish parliament between 1945 and 1980 was, by any measure, a place where land mattered. It passed two land reform laws, diluted one, and buried another, and the share of its minutes devoted to land reform peaked in 1945 and again in 1969--75. Yet the deputies who owned land, and those who represented the countryside, never formed a bloc in the record of its votes. They voted with the cabinet when their party held it and against it when it did not, and they pursued their interests in the spaces the vote left open. That the countryside entered parliament as a stake in the struggle for the state, rather than as a cleavage among its members, suggests that the articulation of social conflict by parties is not a process separate from state formation but one of its integral parts.
