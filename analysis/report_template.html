Turkish Grand National Assembly · named votes on land and agriculture · 1945–1980

Land and Cabinet, 1945–1980

For thirty-five years, Turkish deputies voted on land, credit, crops, forests and the farm agencies as if each vote were a vote of confidence in the cabinet. Landed interests left their mark in debate, in absence and in party exit, never in a recorded vote against their own party. Land redistribution passed only twice, both times when party competition was absent or suspended.

A single record built from the official minutes: Assembly roll calls, deputy-votes and deputies, linked to the Turkish Political Elites Dataset and to the TBMM Albümü biographies, with the 1945 and 1950 land debates read from the speech corpus. Senate votes for 1961–80 are part of the larger collection but are left out of the comparisons here, because senators' parties are not observed.

Six propositions

  1. P1Agrarian roll calls are confidence votes. In every competitive era the same deputies vote yes in government and no in opposition, on the same farm agencies. The 1950 turnover and the 1961–80 switches identify this within deputies.
  2. P2Only exits that cross the cabinet line move votes. Two exits out of the governing party (1955, 1970) reversed deputies' votes on farm budgets. Two exits between opposition parties (1948, 1967) changed nothing.
  3. P3Land and constituency do not predict votes within party. Farmer and landowner deputies, and deputies from rural provinces, vote like their party colleagues in every era. Landed deputies speak far more than their share in land debates.
  4. P4Opponents of land distribution stay away; opponents of land taxes vote no. On five of five distribution and titling laws the opposing side attended less than usual. On all three land-tax votes it attended and voted no. The only distribution vote it attended in force was the one it could win.
  5. P5Redistribution passes only without competition. The 1945 law (single party, 345–0) and the 1973 law (military-supervised cabinets, 238–5) are the only two. Every competitive government diluted, stalled or administered reform instead.
  6. P6Competition makes agriculture partisan, and tutelage suspends it. The share of contested agrarian votes rose from 0% in 1945 to 58% in 1965–71, fell to 30% under the 1971–73 cabinets, and returned to 47% after 1973.

The eras follow the changes of regime and cabinet that matter for the propositions. Every statistic is computed the same way in every era (definitions under the table).

Contested: the losing side holds at least 5% of votes cast. Not yes: no and abstain as a share of votes cast. Took part: share of listed deputies casting a vote. Government yes / opposition yes: yes among votes cast on contested roll calls. Government premium: the mean, over contested roll calls, of the government deputies' yes share minus the opposition deputies'. The same deputies reverse their votes on the same farm agencies when their party enters or leaves the cabinet.

The 1950 estimate adds the two flips together, so its scale is twice that of a single switch. Read per group, the DP rose 95 points and the CHP fell 59. The 1961–80 effect is smaller, probably because coalition partners and technocratic cabinets blur what being in government means. The direction and the mechanism are the same across the two regimes, the two governing parties, and thirty years.

P2Only exits that cross the cabinet line move votes

Four parliamentary splits form a two-by-two comparison. Leaving the governing party turned deputies against the cabinet's farm budgets. Leaving one opposition party for another did nothing.

Estimand. Difference-in-differences in yes on agrarian roll calls: deputies who left the party minus those who stayed, after minus before the split.
Inference. Fisher randomization over which deputies of the party are labelled as leavers (5,000 draws).
Why it is a test. All four groups left their party for policy or personal reasons. Only two of them changed their relation to the cabinet. If agrarian conviction drove the votes, all four exits should show effects.

The 1955 and 1970 exits are fifteen years and two party systems apart, yet both show the same pattern. Leavers who had voted 100% yes with the cabinet fell to 48% (Hürriyet Partisi) and to between 0% and 27% in each period after the split (Demokratik Parti), while the stayers did not move. In 1970 the future DP founders had already broken with the AP on the land tax six months before the split (Fisher p = 5×10−9). That is the one place where an issue preceded the exit, and the issue was taxing land, not distributing it.

Every within-party estimate is close to zero and precise, across a single-party house, a two-party house and a fragmented one. In 1961–80, farmer deputies took part slightly less often in land votes, which is the only behavioural difference. In 1945, landed deputies did not vote differently. P4Opponents of distribution stay away; opponents of taxation vote no

On land distribution and titling, the losing side's instrument was absence. On land taxes, it was a recorded no.

Measure. For each key land vote, the share of the opposing party's listed deputies who took part, divided by that party's own participation on the other agrarian roll calls of the same era (1.0 = usual attendance). Present members' votes are reported alongside.
Coding. "Distribution" transfers or registers land (land laws, titling). "Taxation" taxes land or property. The 1965 motion was procedural, but its defeat stopped İnönü's land reform bill, and the opposition could win it.
VoteKindOpposing partyPresentUsualRatioNo, of present

The rule holds at every one of the nine votes, under three governing parties.

  • Distribution: the side that opposed the bill attended at 0.04–0.69 of its usual rate, five times out of five. In 1973, 2 of the CHP's 101 listed deputies took part.
  • Taxation: the losing side attended at 0.89–1.48 of its usual rate and voted no, 83–100% of those present.
  • The 1945 precedent: the five future DP founders voted no on the 29 May budget (368–5), then were absent from 91% of the votes that followed, the land law included.
  • The exception: the one distribution vote the opposition could win, the 1965 motion, drew the AP at 1.65 times its usual rate. It defeated İnönü's bill 157–167.

The pattern fits a simple account. A recorded no against giving land to peasants is costly with rural voters, and a recorded no against a tax is not. Opponents of redistribution therefore vote no only when the no can win.

P5Redistribution passes only without competition

Land reform passed in only two moments, and in both no opposition party was contesting the cabinet: a single-party house in 1945 and military-supervised cabinets in 1973.

Process tracing gives each passage the same sequence: the opposition is excluded or absent, and the governing majority votes as a bloc. In 1945 the regime was a single party and the dissidents withdrew. In 1973 party conflict was suspended by the military (Proposition 6) and the radical left was closed. Under open competition, the party in power used land policy for its own constituents: dilution before an election in 1950, titling in the 1950s and 1966. The parliamentary record ends there. The fate of the 1973 law afterwards (it was annulled by the Constitutional Court in 1977) left no roll call and is context, not evidence from this record.

P6Competition makes agriculture partisan; tutelage suspends it

The share of agrarian roll calls with a real losing side grew with each step of party competition and fell when the military supervised the cabinets.

  • 1945: no contested vote, and 0.1% of votes cast were not yes. Dissent took the form of absence.
  • 1946–60: a two-party opposition voted no on agency budgets: 11% of roll calls were contested under the CHP and 24% under the DP. The government premium was already 0.72–0.76.
  • 1961–71: coalitions and then an AP majority facing a fragmented opposition. Contested roll calls reached 38% and then 58%.
  • 1971–73: the government premium fell to 0.31, the lowest of any competitive era. The AP–CHP distance on Assembly roll calls dropped by 0.38 after the memorandum (placebo in time, p = 0.03).
  • 1973–80: the premium returned to 0.84, the highest of the period, and 23% of votes cast were not yes.

LIMITSWhat this record cannot show

  • Effects on farmers. Support prices, credit ceilings and most decrees were never named votes.
  • Holdings. The occupation flag does not measure acres. A deputy who owned large estates but listed law as his profession is coded as a lawyer.
  • The Senate. Senators' parties are not observed, so the 1961–80 Senate stays out of the comparisons, and senators' occupations are not coded: the Senate albums are scans without text.
  • P5 rests on two cases. It is a pattern consistent with theory, not an identified effect. Before 1960, the comparison covers 189 roll calls against 322 after.
  • Speech measures. The GNACT text sometimes invents content, so salience is a phrase count and every quotation was checked on the TBMM PDF.
  • Era boundaries. 1946–50 has four contested roll calls; its premium is shown but not leaned on.
