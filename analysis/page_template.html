Grand National Assembly of Turkey · recorded votes on land and agriculture · 1961–1980

Cabinets, Not Countryside

Who decided Turkish agrarian policy in parliament: landlords, rural constituencies, the radical left, ideology, or the cabinet? A test of the big theories with every deputy's recorded vote, their speeches in the minutes, and the sequence of regimes.

Data: roll calls, 182,005 member-votes, read from the TBMM minutes and linked to the Turkish Political Elites Dataset.

The argument

Classic accounts explain agrarian policy by who owns land (Moore, Albertus), by the political weight of rural voters (Bates, Olson), or, in Turkey, by the cleavage between a reforming centre and a rural periphery (Mardin). In the recorded votes of 1961–80 none of these organises the behaviour of deputies. What organises it is the deputy's relation to the cabinet. Within that structure, landed interest surfaces on one issue only: taxing land. And it shows itself through party exit, not through regional voting blocs.

  1. When a deputy's party enters the cabinet, the same deputy becomes points more likely to vote for agricultural agency budgets (DIDM, 95% CI ). The placebo on the preceding pair of votes is points.
  2. Leaving the governing party reverses budget votes: the 21 AP deputies who founded the DP in December 1970 move points against AP stayers (randomization inference p < 0.001).
  3. Landed interest appears on one issue. Six months before the split, the future DP founders already voted against the 1970 property tax while their colleagues voted for it (Fisher's exact test, p = ). A general second voting dimension measured before the split does not predict exit.
  4. A party's vote follows its stance toward the cabinet, not its coalition partner's ideology: the MSP backed the land reform agency with the centre-left CHP and again with the right-wing AP.
  5. The 1971 military memorandum suspended partisan conflict for a year: the AP–CHP distance fell by points (placebo-in-time p = ). Conflict returned first on taxing land.
  6. A second exit that left government status unchanged changed nothing: the CHP deputies who founded the Güven Partisi in 1967 moved points against CHP stayers (p = ). Paired with the AP→DP exit, this isolates government status from the party label.
  7. The radical flank was loud, isolated and never decisive. The Workers' Party (TİP) proposed land ceilings and state purchasing, and stood alone against both large parties on 8 agrarian roll calls. It could not reverse a single outcome among , though its presence supplied the quorum for that passed. Land reform came only after the party was closed and the military demanded reform.

1. What is observed

The data are the open, by-name votes (açık oylama) of the Millet Meclisi and the Cumhuriyet Senatosu on land and agriculture. Every list printed in the minutes was read from the TBMM PDFs and matched to deputies. of roll calls reproduce the printed tallies exactly and are within two names. All 73 land votes are exact, 13 of them after checks against the page images. A hand audit found 73 of 75 sampled links correct.

Two features of the data shape every claim below.

  • Open votes were compulsory for budgets and final accounts, and taken on request otherwise. Budgets therefore dominate (281 of 567 roll calls), and the most consequential agrarian instruments of the period are not observed at all: support prices, credit ceilings, and laws passed by show of hands.
  • Party at the time of the vote is exact for deputies who never switched and dated by the known splits for the 21% of member-votes cast by switchers. Senators' parties are not observed, so the causal designs use the Assembly.

2. The space of agrarian conflict

For each Assembly term I estimate a two-dimensional item-response model of yes/no votes. This is the Bayesian model of Clinton, Jackman and Rivers (2004), fitted at its posterior mode, a probabilistic cousin of NOMINATE. It uses roll calls whose lists are within two names of the tally and on which at least 2.5% voted in the minority. The model classifies 97–99% of votes correctly. It reduces errors over a majority-vote baseline by 0.83–0.99 (APRE).

Two facts follow.

  • The land reform law is almost a pure first-dimension vote (7°): land redistribution was fought along the government–opposition line.
  • Titling and property-tax votes load most on the second dimension (39° and 23°): conflicts over property claims cut across parties.

The second dimension has no regional content. Its within-party correlation with the rural share of the province and with the East/Southeast is below 0.1 in every term.

An earlier fit on all roll calls produced a second dimension that tracked the alphabetical order of provinces (r = 0.41 in 1969–73). That is a parsing artifact from a few votes whose sections the OCR mixed up. Restricting to well-measured votes removes it (|r| ≤ 0.05).

3. Claim 1: the cabinet causes agrarian budget votes

Estimand. The average effect, on deputies whose party enters (or leaves) the cabinet between two consecutive agricultural budget roll calls of the same term, of that change on the probability of voting yes among votes cast. Leaving is counted with the sign reversed.
Design. The DIDM estimator of de Chaisemartin and D'Haultfœuille (2020). It compares the switchers' change to that of deputies with the same prior status whose status did not change. This avoids the negative weights of two-way fixed effects when effects differ across switches. Cabinets of 1971–75 without party mandates are excluded, as are ministers, members on leave and presiding officers.
Identifying assumption. Parallel trends between switchers and stayers across consecutive budget votes, and no anticipation. Tested with the same comparison on the preceding pair of votes.
Inference. Bootstrap over deputies (499 draws). Status changes happen at the party-by-cabinet level, so the effective number of independent shocks is smaller than the number of deputies. Read the intervals as lower bounds on uncertainty.

Going into government raises the yes probability by points and turnout by points, for the same people voting on the same agency's budget. On farm benefits the logic is different: almost nobody votes against them. On the 18 benefit votes that failed for lack of quorum, government deputies were points less likely to be present than opposition deputies on the same vote, excused absences excluded. That comparison is within the same vote, not within the same deputy, so it is descriptive.

4. Claim 2: party exit reverses the vote

Estimand. The effect on the 21 AP deputies who founded the DP on 18 December 1970 (the average effect on the treated) of leaving the governing party for an opposition party, on the probability of voting yes on contested agricultural budget roll calls in the 1969–73 Assembly.
Design. Difference-in-differences against the AP deputies who stayed, with an event study by budget roll call. Inference by Fisher randomization over which AP deputies are labelled switchers (5,000 draws).
Scope. Switching was chosen, so the estimate describes these deputies under this exit. It is not the effect of a randomly assigned label.

Claim 3: where landed interest shows

The future DP founders voted with AP on every budget until the day they left. On taxing land they had already broken with it.

  • Of the future founders, voted no and voted yes.
  • Of the deputies who stayed in AP, voted no and voted yes.

The disagreement over land preceded the party split, so preferences over landed property sorted deputies into parties; party membership did not create the preference.

The preference was issue-specific. A second dimension estimated only from pre-split votes does not predict who left (|r| = 0.08, within-party permutation p = 0.19).

5. Claim 4: stance toward the cabinet, not the partner's ideology

The National Salvation Party (MSP) governed first with the centre-left CHP (1974), then with the right-wing AP (1975–77). If parties voted on land reform by ideology, or followed their partner's, its votes on the land reform agency's budget would change between these coalitions. They do not.

Of the same 31 MSP deputies, 29 voted yes alongside the CHP in 1974 and again alongside the AP in 1976. The comparison is within party and largely within person, but it is a single party, so it is a case rather than an estimate.

6. Two exits: separating the party label from the cabinet

Claim 2 shows that leaving AP reversed budget votes. But that exit changed two things at once: the deputies' party, and their relation to the cabinet, since they moved from the governing party into opposition. A second split in the same period changed only the first.

In May 1967 the right wing of the CHP left to found the Güven Partisi (GP), in protest at the party's turn "left of centre", itself a response to the TİP. Both the CHP and the new GP sat in opposition to the AP government. Before the split, the future GP founders voted exactly like their CHP colleagues on agrarian questions: against the 1966 titling law (13 no, 0 yes, against the stayers' 41 no and 1 yes).

Estimand. The effect on the CHP deputies who founded the GP of leaving the CHP for another opposition party, on the probability of voting yes on contested agricultural budget and accounts roll calls (1965–69). The comparison group is the CHP stayers.
Design. The same as for AP→DP: difference-in-differences with Fisher randomization inference (5,000 draws).

The comparison is a paired, most-similar design. Both are exits by a faction of a major party, in the same decade, scored on the same kind of roll call. They differ in whether the exit moved deputies across the government–opposition line. The party label alone moved budget votes by points, a null. The label combined with leaving government moved them by .

7. The radical flank: what TİP changed

The Workers' Party of Turkey (TİP) entered parliament in 1965 with 15 deputies, elected under the national-remainder system. It was the only party that demanded redistribution of land in radical terms. Its deputies used the floor to propose ceilings on land and state control of crop purchasing.

Roll callTİP yes–noAP yesCHP yes

Roll calls of 1965–69 on which TİP's majority voted against the majorities of both AP and CHP. On the 1966 titling law TİP voted no (0–12), together with the CHP.

The recorded votes measure what this rhetoric achieved in parliament.

  • Position. TİP is the most extreme bloc in the 1965–69 scaling: its mean position is −1.12, against −0.65 for the CHP and +0.34 for the AP.
  • Influence on outcomes. In none of the roll calls of 1965–69 could TİP's votes have reversed the result.
  • Influence on quorum. TİP's presence was decisive for the 226-voter quorum on roll calls, of which passed. Its no votes on the 1969 Cooperatives Law, for example, helped make the quorum for a law it opposed. The quorum rule (226 voters in a 450-seat Assembly) agrees with of recorded outcomes.
  • Attendance. TİP deputies cast a vote on of the roll calls they could have voted on.

The direct effect of the radical flank on recorded agrarian outcomes was nil. Its ceiling amendments were put to a show of hands and not adopted, so no recorded vote exists on them. The titling law it opposed then passed with the AP's majority. What changed the policy was the sequence that followed: the military memorandum of March 1971, the closure of the TİP by the Constitutional Court in July 1971, and a land reform law passed in 1973 by the same AP that had voted down İnönü's bill in 1965 (§8). That sequence fits the argument that land reform under military tutelage is pre-emptive, made to defuse the radical left rather than in response to its votes (Huntington 1968; Albertus 2015). The roll calls can rule out the direct route. They cannot by themselves prove the pre-emptive one.

The comparison by agreement and difference points to one condition present only in 1971–73: party competition was suspended by the military while the radical left was excluded.

  • Under open competition, a centre-left government (1965) and a right-wing majority facing a radical flank (1965–69) both failed to legislate land reform.
  • After 1974, competition returned, and the reform's administration became an object of cabinet politics.

9. Claim 5: military tutelage and the suspension of party conflict

Estimand. The change in the mean absolute difference between the AP and CHP yes-shares on Assembly roll calls, from the Demirel government (October 1969 – March 1971) to the period after the memorandum of 12 March 1971 (to April 1972). The same parliament and the same deputies throughout.
Inference. Placebo in time: the same before/after contrast at break dates in the other terms (30 roll calls on each side). The p-value is the share of placebo drops at least as large.
Threat. The end of the consensus coincides with Ecevit's takeover of the CHP (May 1972) and the 1973 election campaign. Only the onset is attributed to the memorandum.

Under the post-memorandum cabinets of Erim, partisan distance collapsed to near zero, including on the 1971 property-tax amendment. The collapse was deepest when the CHP held no cabinet posts (Erim II), so shared office does not explain it. The first issue on which conflict returned, in June 1972, was again the taxation of land.

Section 8 places this episode in the longer sequence.

In June 1973 the land reform law passed with the AP's votes. The CHP did not vote against it; 103 of its 105 deputies stayed away. Abstention was the opposition's instrument on land, as quorum denial was the government's on farm benefits.

11. What this does not show

  • Any effect on farmers. The decisive agrarian instruments of the period were decrees and show-of-hands laws that left no individual record.
  • Senate behaviour by party, which is not observed.
  • Effects of being in government on land-tax votes. No deputy's status changed between those votes, so the estimand has no support.
  • Personal landholding. Occupation in the elites data is too coarse, so landed interest is inferred from behaviour on land votes, not from assets.

Methods appendix

Ideal points. P(yij = 1) = Λ(αj + xi′βj), with xi ∈ ℝ². The model is fitted at its posterior mode with priors x ~ N(0, I) and α, β ~ N(0, 25), separately per Assembly term. The points are rotated to their principal axes. Roll calls enter if they are within two names of the printed tally, have at least 2.5% in the minority and at least 20 voters. Deputies enter with at least half the term's scaled roll calls (at most 10 required).

DIDM. For each consecutive pair of budget roll calls (t−1, t) within a term, switchers 0→1 are compared with stayers 0→0, and 1→0 with 1→1. Cell estimates are averaged with weights equal to the number of switchers. The placebo repeats the comparison on (t−2, t−1) for the same groups.

Randomization inference. The treated set (21 DP founders) is reassigned at random among the AP deputies of 1969–73, and the difference-in-differences recomputed 5,000 times. None of the draws reached the observed effect.
